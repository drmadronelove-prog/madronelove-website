    Module 01

    Four Ideas That Do Most of the Work

    If you read nothing else, read this page. Everything in the other fifteen modules is one of these four ideas applied to a specific problem.

    You have had a dog for two weeks, you have never had a dog, and he arrived with a full set of behaviors you did not choose. That is a normal starting position and it is not a hole you dig out of with willpower. It is a hole you get out of with a small number of correct concepts, applied boringly, for about eight weeks.

    One. Behavior is not character. Behavior is consequences.

    Earthwind is not stubborn, dominant, spiteful, guilty, or testing you. Those are stories humans tell about behavior. What he actually is, is an animal doing whatever has worked recently. Pulling on the leash works, because pulling makes the interesting thing get closer. Barking at a stranger works, because the stranger always eventually leaves. Nipping works, because it makes the game get faster.

    So the question is never "why is he like this." The question is what is paying for this, and what could I pay for instead.

    Myth to drop on day one

    Dominance and "being the alpha." This comes from 1940s studies of unrelated captive wolves in a zoo enclosure, and the researcher who popularized it, L. David Mech, has spent decades publicly trying to retract it. Wild wolf packs are families. Dogs are not wolves, and your dog is not running a coup. Every technique built on this idea (alpha rolls, pinning, staring him down, eating first, going through doors first) has no evidence behind it and a measurable association with aggressive responses.67

    Two. You cannot train a dog who is over threshold.

    There is a level of arousal above which a dog physically cannot learn. Not "will not." Cannot. Blood is routed to muscle, the stress response is running, and the parts of the brain that do associations are not the parts in charge. Above that line, every repetition is wasted, and worse, he is rehearsing the panic.

    Practically: most training failures are actually distance failures. He isn't ignoring you, he's too close to the thing. Module 04 teaches you to see the line and Module 10 teaches you to work under it.

    Three. Management is not cheating. It is half the plan.

    Management means arranging the world so the behavior can't happen: a fan by the door so he doesn't hear footsteps, a baby gate so he can't rush guests, a long line so a recall can't fail. Novice owners feel like management is avoiding the real work. It isn't. Every rehearsal of a bad behavior makes it stronger, so preventing rehearsals is doing the work. Training is slow; management is instant, and it buys you the calm weeks in which training can happen.

    Four. Reward-based training is the method with the evidence.

    This is not a philosophical preference. It is the position of the American Veterinary Society of Animal Behavior, which recommends reward-based methods exclusively and states that aversive methods should not be used.12 The research is consistent: dogs trained with aversive methods show more stress signals during training, higher cortisol, and a more pessimistic outlook in cognitive bias tests weeks later, with no efficiency advantage.345 A survey of 140 owners of dogs with existing behavior problems found that confrontational methods produced an aggressive response a large fraction of the time: hitting or kicking (43%), growling at the dog (41%), alpha roll (31%), staring down (30%).6

    For a dog who is already wary of strangers, this matters double. Punishing a fear response teaches the dog that the scary thing predicts pain, which is the exact opposite of the association you need.

    Earthwind barks and lunges at a stranger on the sidewalk. Which response is most likely to make it worse next week?

    Correction adds an unpleasant event to a situation that already unsettles him, which strengthens "strangers are bad" and can suppress the warning signals without touching the feeling underneath. Leaving is management and it works immediately. Feeding is counterconditioning and it's the long game. Both are in Module 10.

    What this manual assumes

    • You want a dog who is comfortable, not a dog who is obedient. Obedience is a side effect of comfort.
    • You will do about ten minutes a day, in two or three pieces, most days. Not an hour on Saturday.
    • You will get some of this wrong. Dogs are extremely forgiving of inconsistent humans and extremely unforgiving of frightening ones.

    Name it concretely. "Runs up to strangers barking, won't stop until they leave" is workable. "He's reactive" is not.

    Module 02

    The Two-Week Reset

    Before any protocol in this manual will work, he needs to come down. This module is almost entirely subtraction, it takes two weeks, and it is the highest-return thing in the book.

    The problem this fixes

    The standard advice given to every new dog owner is: get him out, socialise him, meet people, tire him out, dog park. That advice was written for an eight-week-old puppy in a stable home. Applied to a ten-month-old adolescent who moved house two weeks ago, it produces a dog whose stress hormones never return to baseline, whose threshold drops a little further every day, and who then looks like he is getting worse rather than better.

    If he is barking at more things than he was in week one, sleeping badly, chewing everything, unable to settle, and failing at cues he used to know, the first hypothesis is not that the training isn't working. It is that he never gets back to zero.

    The mechanism, in one paragraph

    Adrenaline and cortisol from a high-arousal episode take hours to clear, and they stack across days.37 Wednesday's dog park is still in him on Thursday. A week of daily overstimulation means he is starting every morning already partway up the meter, so the thing that produced no reaction in week one produces a reaction now. This is called trigger stacking and Module 04 has a slider that lets you feel the shape of it.

    The single most useful distinction in this manual

    There are two separate budgets and almost everyone spends them as if they were one.

    The activity budget

    How much his body needs to do. It is large. A ten-month-old adolescent needs a great deal of physical and mental work and will be miserable without it.

    The exposure budget

    How much novelty his nervous system can process before his threshold drops. Right now it is very small, and it is being spent entirely on strangers.

    The limit on a walk is not minutes. It is how many novel humans are in it. Forty minutes on an empty trail is fine and good. Fifteen minutes on a busy sidewalk is not. When you hear "shorten the walks" in this manual, that means shorten the exposure, not the exercise. Spend the activity budget somewhere the exposure budget isn't being touched.

    Why tiring him out backfires

    Exercise raises arousal

    It does not lower it. A physically exhausted dog with a hot nervous system has less impulse control, not more. That is the version most likely to have a meltdown at the end of the day.

    You are building an athlete

    Sustained repetitive exercise increases his conditioning. The same walk does less next month, so you walk further, and you have entered an arms race you lose. Working-dog people know this one well.

    The chemistry outlasts the walk

    See above. The residue is the problem, not the effort.

    Overtired looks identical to under-exercised

    Frantic, mouthy, can't settle, ignores cues. The novice response is another walk. The correct response is a nap.

    You are aiming for calm, not tired. They are different systems and only one of them is your problem.

    What actually lowers arousal

    • 01Sniffing. Nasal work is genuinely fatiguing and it is parasympathetic. Twenty minutes of a dog reading one hedge does more for him than three miles of pavement, and it has the research behind it.20
    • 02Chewing and licking. Rhythmic oral behavior, same system. See Module 13 for what to give him and how to make it last.
    • 03Foraging. Scatter his dinner in the grass instead of using a bowl. Ten minutes of work, no prep, no extra calories.
    • 04Sleep. Sixteen to eighteen hours. The under-slept dog is the one who reads as hyperactive. This is free and it is probably the highest-leverage item on the page.1213

    Two weeks of subtraction

    For fourteen days, the plan is to remove novelty rather than add training. You are not falling behind. You are creating the conditions in which the rest of this manual becomes possible.

    No dog park

    For this dog specifically it is the worst environment available, and not because it is overwhelming. Every human there is someone he will never see again, so he barks, they go, and the habituation curve never once completes. He is running dozens of reps a week of "bark until the novel human is gone." If he needs dog time, use the neighbour's dog.

    No visits, no guests, no new places

    Including the well-meant ones. Including your family. Two weeks.

    Same short route, quiet hours

    Two outings of ten to fifteen minutes on the quietest street you have, at the times fewest people are on it. Same route daily, so the route stops being new information.

    Spend the activity budget where there is no novelty

    Play with the neighbour's dog, as much as both dogs want. Long sniff walks in genuinely empty places, any length you like. Five-minute training sessions three or four times a day. Tug and flirt pole in short bursts. Food games indoors.

    Enforced naps

    One to two hours, twice a day, in a quiet room behind a gate or pen. He will not take these voluntarily. Most household chaos in the first month is sleep debt.

    A chew or lick session daily

    Not enrichment theatre. It is the parasympathetic system, on purpose, once a day.

    Give it five to seven days before you judge it

    Some of the energy you are seeing right now is cortisol that has not cleared. If after a week of real dog play and real sleep he is genuinely climbing the walls, then this diagnosis was wrong and you adjust. That is a real test, not something you have to take on faith.

    What you add back, and in what order

    After two weeks, add one variable at a time and watch what his threshold does for two or three days before adding the next.     He has enormous energy and two fifteen-minute walks feels obviously insufficient. What is the correct read?

    He does need a lot of activity. He just cannot afford to buy it with novel humans right now. Every option in step four of the protocol above burns energy without spending exposure, and dog play with a familiar dog is the most efficient of them.

    Write down what he is like today, so you can tell in two weeks whether this worked.

    Module 03

    How Learning Works

    The actual mechanics. This is the module that makes every other module make sense, and it takes about fifteen minutes to understand.

    There are exactly two kinds of learning here

    Classical: what things predict

    Involuntary. He doesn't choose it. Hallway noise predicts a scary stranger, so hallway noise itself becomes scary. This is where fear lives, and where you fix fear.

    Operant: what behavior pays

    Voluntary. He chooses it. Sitting produces cheese, so sitting increases. This is where skills live: sit, come, walk nicely, go to your mat.

    Almost every mistake novice owners make is treating a classical problem (fear, reactivity, crate panic) as an operant one (disobedience). You cannot ask a dog to choose not to be afraid. You change what the thing predicts, and the feeling changes underneath.

    The operant half: four boxes

    NameWhat happensEffectExample
    Positive reinforcementYou add something goodBehavior increasesHe sits, cheese appears. More sitting.
    Negative reinforcementYou remove something badBehavior increasesLeash pressure stops when he moves. He moves to end pressure.
    Positive punishmentYou add something badBehavior decreasesLeash pop for lunging.
    Negative punishmentYou remove something goodBehavior decreasesTeeth touch skin, the game ends.

    "Positive" and "negative" here mean added and subtracted, not good and bad. You will live almost entirely in the first box and occasionally in the fourth. The fourth is how you handle nipping: the fun stops, nothing unpleasant is added.

    Why his barking is so durable

    Barking at a stranger is maintained by negative reinforcement, the second row of that table: the barking removes something he wants removed. The person always eventually leaves, so it works every single time. There is no more reliable schedule than that, which is why this behavior is stronger than anything else he does.

    The marker: the most useful thirty seconds in this manual

    Dogs learn from consequences that arrive within roughly a second of the behavior. You cannot get cheese out of your pocket in one second. So you need a sound that means that, right there, is what earned it, food is coming.

    Pick one: a clicker, or the word "yes" said crisply, always the same way. Then build it:

    Charge the marker

    Sit with twenty pieces of food. Say "yes," then hand him a piece. Wait a few seconds. Repeat. He does nothing to earn it. You are teaching the word, not a behavior.

    Ready when: he looks at your hand the instant you say it

    Mark something he already does

    Wait for him to sit. The instant his rear touches down, "yes," then feed. Say nothing else. No cue yet.

    Ready when: he offers sits deliberately to make you say it

    Add the word

    Now that sitting is a thing he does on purpose, say "sit" just as he begins to do it. After a week the word comes first.

    Ready when: he sits on the word alone, eight times out of ten

    Notice the order: behavior first, name it second. Novices say the word over and over at a dog who has no idea what it means, which teaches him the word means nothing. Get the behavior, then label it.

    The one-second rule

    Marker timing is the difference between training and confusion. If he sits, you fumble, and you mark two seconds later while he's standing up, you just paid for standing up. Mark late, feed late; that's fine. Just make sure the sound lands on the behavior.

    Reinforcement schedules, and why he gets worse before he gets better

    Early on, pay every single repetition. Once a behavior is fluent, you shift to paying unpredictably: sometimes, not always. Unpredictable payment is more durable than constant payment, for the same reason slot machines are more compelling than vending machines.

    Related, and important: the extinction burst. When you stop paying a behavior that used to work, it does not fade quietly. It first gets louder, longer, and more intense, because that has worked before. The dog who barked for attention and got ignored barks harder for two days. If you cave on day two, you have just taught him that the price is now two days of barking. Decide before you start whether you can hold, because a half-finished extinction makes the behavior much worse than never starting.

    Habituation, which is the other thing that is happening

    Habituation is the simplest kind of learning there is: a stimulus repeats, nothing happens, and the response fades on its own. No food, no marker, no technique. It is how he stopped reacting to your refrigerator, and it is how he stopped barking at you after the first day.

    It matters here because habituation only completes if the stimulus stays long enough and comes back often enough. A person he sees once and never again cannot habituate. A person he sees six times over three weeks does. Module 10 is built almost entirely on this fact.

    Four more concepts you will use constantly

    Dogs don't generalize

    A sit learned in your kitchen is a kitchen sit. He is not being defiant outdoors, he genuinely has not learned that one yet. Retrain every skill in every new room, then outside, then outside with distractions. Budget for this.

    Premack principle

    A high-probability behavior reinforces a low-probability one. Translation: "do this boring thing and you get the thing you actually want." Check in with me, then go sniff. This is the single best tool for a dog who wants to sniff instead of walk.

    Capturing, luring, shaping

    Capturing: wait for it, mark it. Luring: guide with food, then fade the food fast or it becomes a bribe. Shaping: pay successive approximations, the way you'd play hot-and-cold. Shaping builds the most confident dogs.

    Poisoned cues

    If a word has ever predicted something unpleasant, it stays contaminated. Dogs who ignore "come" usually learned that come predicts the end of fun or a bath. If you've burned a word, get a new word.

    Sessions

    Two to five minutes. Ten to twenty repetitions. End while he still wants more, not when he's had enough. Two three-minute sessions a day beat one twenty-minute session by a wide margin, and a poodle will get bored and start offering nonsense long before you get bored.

    Two or three minutes immediately before each meal is the easiest slot to actually keep, because the motivation is already in the room. And end every session with the same release word — “all done,” hands open, a bit of fuss — so there is a clear edge to it. Without one he keeps offering behaviors at you, gets no reply, and the last thing he practises is being wrong.

    Earthwind sits perfectly in the kitchen. On the sidewalk he stares past you and ignores "sit." What is happening?

    Skills are context-specific and degrade as arousal rises. The fix is to lower the difficulty, not raise the pressure: a quieter street, more distance from the trigger, better food, and a few easy reps to rebuild.

    Module 04

    Reading Him

    Dogs communicate continuously and almost entirely with their bodies. Learning to read it is the highest-leverage skill in this manual, because every protocol here depends on you knowing whether he is under threshold right now.

    Arousal is one system

    Excitement and fear and aggression are not separate wiring. They are the same activation, differently flavored. A dog spiking with joy has exactly as little impulse control as a dog spiking with fear, which is why the friendliest moment of the day is often where the nipping happens.

    Below a certain level of arousal he can think, hear cues, and choose. Above it he cannot. That line is his threshold, and no training exists above it.

    The one reliable field test

    Will he eat? A dog who takes food, chews it, and swallows normally is under threshold. A dog who ignores food he normally loves, or takes it and drops it, or takes it hard enough to hurt, is over. This works everywhere: in the crate, on the sidewalk, with a stranger approaching. It is more reliable than how calm he looks.

    The ladder

    Dogs escalate through a predictable sequence of signals when uncomfortable, sometimes called the ladder of aggression or the ladder of communication.89 They start at the bottom. They only climb when the lower rungs get ignored.

    A note on Earthwind specifically

    He does not growl before he barks, and he has never shown teeth. That is not a warning sign in itself, and a dog who approaches people voluntarily and is friendly once he settles is not on a bite trajectory. What it does mean is that his ladder is short: he goes from noticing to a loud forward display without the middle rungs. So do not build a plan that depends on reading an early growl. Use distance and the food test instead, both of which are available to you before anything escalates.

    Never punish a growl

    A growl is a gift. It is a dog telling you, in advance and without damage, that he needs something to change. Punish it and you don't remove the feeling, you remove the warning, and the next escalation arrives with nothing in front of it. Dogs described as biting "with no warning" are very often dogs who were corrected for growling. When he growls: note what caused it, calmly increase distance, and put that trigger on your list to work on.

    The vocabulary, concretely

    Whale eye

    Whites of the eyes showing in a crescent, usually while his head is turned away but his eyes stay on the thing. Strong discomfort. Very common when someone leans over a dog.

    Tongue flick

    A fast lick of the nose with no food anywhere. One of the earliest and most missed stress signals.

    Shake off

    A full-body shake like he's wet, when he is dry. A reset. Usually means a stressful moment just ended. Good sign.

    Loose vs stiff

    Look at the whole outline. A comfortable dog is a series of curves and his weight is even. A worried dog is straight lines: stiff legs, level topline, weight shifted forward or back.

    The tail is not a mood ring

    Wagging means arousal, not friendliness. High and fast and stiff is a warning. Low, loose, whole-rear-end wag is friendly. Read it together with the mouth and eyes.

    Mouth

    Open, loose, corners soft, tongue relaxed: fine. Closed suddenly and tight, corners pulled forward: not fine, and it usually happens a second before something else.

    Trigger stacking

    Stress hormones do not clear instantly. Each new stressor lands on top of the residue of the last one, and arousal ratchets rather than resetting.37 This is why the dog who was fine with the delivery guy at ten is unreachable with the neighbor at six. A dog who reacts badly at 6pm was usually climbing since noon. After a genuinely big event, assume it takes the rest of the day, and give him a quiet one.

    Nothing yet. He's asleep on the rug and life is good.

    Settling versus shutting down

    Both look like a still dog. They are opposites. A settling dog has come down: he sighs, stretches out, his weight goes into the floor, he'll take a treat. A shut-down dog has run out of options and gone rigid: tucked, tight, hypervigilant eyes, refusing food. Shut-down dogs get labeled "so good in the crate" right up until they aren't, and the next signal comes with less warning.

    When he's over threshold

    Do not train, correct, or reason. Cut the input. Distance, or a quiet room, or the crate if he likes it. Then let twenty minutes pass. You are not giving up on the session, you are making the next one possible.

    Module 05

    Ten Months Old, Two Weeks In

    Two things are happening at once and both of them make him look worse than he is. Knowing the shape of it will save you from concluding, in week three, that you adopted the wrong dog.

    Thing one: he is a teenager

    Dog adolescence is real and it is measurable. A study following the same dogs through puberty found that at around eight months of age they were significantly less likely to obey a cue from their own caregiver, and harder to train, while obedience to a stranger did not drop the same way. The effect was strongest in dogs with a more insecure attachment to their person.10 It resolves. It just resolves at eighteen months, not next week.

    Earthwind is about ten months old. He is squarely in it. Practically this means: cues that worked yesterday will fail today, he will be more reactive to novelty than he was at five months, and the impulse control is not there yet because the brain doing the controlling is not finished.

    Thing two: he just moved

    His entire frame of reference was deleted two weeks ago. Everything he knew about who feeds him, where he sleeps, what noises mean, and which humans are safe is gone.

    You will hear the "3-3-3 rule" (three days to decompress, three weeks to settle, three months to feel at home). It's a useful shape and it has no research behind it. The research that does exist is less comforting and more useful: a study tracking 99 adopted shelter dogs with a validated behavior questionnaire at 7, 30, 90 and 180 days found that several problem categories increased over the first six months, including stranger-directed aggression, excitability, touch sensitivity, chasing, and training difficulty, while separation-related behavior and attention-seeking decreased. Owner-directed and dog-directed aggression didn't change. Despite all that, 93.7% of owners rated their dog's overall behavior as excellent or good at six months.11

    What that means for you

    The reactivity toward strangers is likely to look worse at week six than it does now, and that is the documented normal trajectory of a settling dog, not evidence that you're failing. It means you should run the reset in Module 02 now and start the work in Module 10 as soon as it ends, rather than waiting to see if it resolves.

    The honeymoon, and its end

    Days 1–7

    Overwhelmed. Sometimes eerily well behaved, sometimes shut down. Not his personality. Do almost nothing but feed him, walk him, and let him sleep.

    Weeks 2–4

    The real dog arrives. Opinions, barking, testing, guarding a spot on the couch. This reads as regression. It is the opposite: he has relaxed enough to have preferences.

    Months 2–3

    He starts to believe he lives here. Routines stick. Training begins to hold across contexts. This is when your work starts visibly paying.

    Months 12–18

    Adolescence winds down. Impulse control arrives. The dog you have been building shows up.

    Sleep is a training intervention

    Young dogs sleep a great deal, and adolescent dogs still need substantially more rest than adults; the descriptive cohort data on dogs up to twelve months shows daytime sleep declining across the first year while night sleep lengthens.12 In shelter dogs, more daytime resting is associated with better welfare, not worse.13

    The practical version: an overtired dog looks exactly like an under-exercised one. Frantic, mouthy, can't settle, ignores cues. The novice response is another walk, which makes it worse. The correct response is an enforced nap: crate or pen or tether, in a quiet room, one to two hours. Most household chaos in the first month is sleep debt.

    Week three. He's suddenly barking at things he ignored on day two, and his sit has fallen apart. What's the most likely explanation?

    Week three is when the honeymoon ends in almost every adoption, and eight to ten months is the documented low point for caregiver-directed obedience. Nothing has broken. Keep the routine boring and keep paying him.

    You will not remember this accurately in six weeks, and you will want to.

    Module 06

    The Crate, From Panic

    He cries and panics in it. That means the crate currently predicts something bad, and the entire job is changing what it predicts. This is a classical problem, not an obedience problem, and it takes weeks.

    Before anything else

    Stop shutting him in there for now. Every panicked minute is a repetition that strengthens "crate means panic," and you cannot outrun that with good sessions later. Use a pen, a gated room, or a tether next to you for management this week, and rebuild the crate from zero on the side.

    A crate is not compulsory

    It is a management tool, and it's a genuinely useful one: vet stays, travel, injury recovery, and a safe place to decompress all go better if a dog crates happily. But if after eight honest weeks he still hates it, an exercise pen or a dog-proofed room with a baby gate does the same job for daily life. Do not turn this into a battle of principle. Module 13 has the direct answer on where he should actually be sleeping tonight and where he should be while you're at work.

    Set it up so it can work

    • Placement. Away from the front door, backed into a corner, out of the traffic path, but in the room where people are. The front door is where every hallway noise and every scent arrives; a crate parked next to it keeps him working all day instead of sleeping.
    • Two crates is fine and good. Bedroom for night, living room for departures. Give each a consistent job.
    • Size. He should be able to stand, turn around, and lie fully stretched out. Bigger than that is fine for an adult dog who is house-trained.
    • Cover it partially if it helps him disengage from visual triggers, but leave one side open so it doesn't feel like a trap.
    • Door. Prop it fully open or take it off entirely for the first stage, so it can't swing shut and startle him.
    • White noise or a fan nearby. It masks hallway footsteps, which for a dog reactive to people is doing real work.
    • Never use it as a punishment, and never call him to you and then put him in it. Both poison it.

    The protocol

    Each stage takes as many days as it takes. The rule for advancing is the same every time: he is eating comfortably and voluntarily choosing to be there. If a stage produces stress signals, you went too fast; drop back a stage, and next time split the difference.

    Make it furniture

    Door off or fixed open. Walk past it and drop a piece of food in, several times a day, saying nothing. He can go in or not. You are not looking at him. Do this for two or three days before anything else.

    Advance when: he checks the crate on his own

    Feed at the threshold

    Put his meal in a bowl just inside the opening, where he can eat with his front feet in and his body out. No pressure to go further.

    Advance when: he eats without hesitating

    Move the bowl back

    Over several meals, move it an inch or two further in each time, until he's eating at the back wall with his whole body inside.

    Advance when: he walks in and eats with a loose body

    Ping-pong

    Toss a treat in. He goes in, eats, comes out. Toss another. In and out, twenty times, door never touched. This is the game that builds a dog who likes going in, because leaving is always allowed.

    Advance when: he's running in eagerly

    Name it

    Now that entering is a fluent behavior, say your word ("crate," "bed," anything) just as he commits to going in. The word attaches to a thing he's already doing. Never use the word to make him do it.

    Advance when: he goes in on the word alone

    Touch the door

    He's inside eating. Touch the door with one finger. Feed. Don't move it. Then swing it an inch and back. Feed. You are building a door that predicts food, not confinement.

    Advance when: door movement doesn't make him look up

    Close for one second

    Push it shut, don't latch, immediately open. Feed. Then two seconds. Then one second again. Vary it. Never build in a straight line; a session that only ever gets harder teaches him to dread the next rep.

    Advance when: 30 seconds closed, calm, taking food

    Latch it, with something great

    Now the frozen Kong or lick mat goes in with him and the door latches. You sit right there. He finishes or gets bored, you open the door. The food ends when the door opens, not before.

    Advance when: he settles rather than working the door

    You get boring

    Same as above but you read a book across the room. Then you leave the room for five seconds and come back. Then thirty. Then you make a cup of tea. Ordinary, unremarkable comings and goings.

    Advance when: he doesn't track you to the doorway

    Attach it to sleep

    Crate beside your bed at night, door closed, you within arm's reach. Nighttime is much easier than daytime departures because you're there. Most dogs get this stage weeks before they get real alone-time.

    Advance when: he sleeps through

    0 of 10

    Five things that undo weeks of work

    • 01Letting him out while he's screaming. You just paid, generously, for screaming. Wait for a gap: two seconds of quiet, even a breath, then open. If you truly cannot wait, get him out, but then make the next session dramatically easier so it doesn't happen again.
    • 02Lifting or pushing him in. Not cruel, just counterproductive: it teaches him that hands near the crate mean being put somewhere, and he starts avoiding you when you approach it.
    • 03Big emotional departures and reunions. A dramatic goodbye tells him something significant is happening. Leave like you're getting the mail.
    • 04Crating him wired. Crate after a sniffy walk and a meal, not after a wrestling session. You are asking his body to do something it currently can't.
    • 05Testing the limit. The temptation to see if he can do an hour today ends every good week. Increase duration in small varied steps and stay well under his ceiling.

    What goes in there

    Frozen stuffed Kong

    Soaked kibble plus a spoon of plain yogurt, plain pumpkin, or xylitol-free peanut butter. Freeze solid. 20–40 minutes of licking, which is a self-soothing behavior. Full recipes and the difficulty ladder are in Module 13.

    Lick mat

    Smeared and frozen, suctioned to the crate wall or floor. Cheaper, faster to prep, easier to clean.

    Long chew

    Bully stick, beef trachea, gullet, supervised. Chewing lowers arousal. Skip antlers, hooves, hard cheese chews and nylon; slab fractures on molars are a common and expensive vet finding.

    Nothing, eventually

    The goal is a dog who sleeps in there without a job. Food is scaffolding. Fade it once duration is easy, or you'll build a dog who only tolerates the crate while food lasts.

    Read the leftovers

    An untouched Kong is data. He was over threshold the whole time. Shorten the next session rather than lengthening it, no matter how quiet the camera looked.

    How long is reasonable

    Overnight

    Fine at ten months once he's comfortable. He can physically hold it seven to eight hours. Crate beside your bed, not across the apartment.

    Daytime

    Four to five hours is the usual working ceiling for an adolescent, and that's after the training is done. A full workday needs a walker, a midday trip, or daycare.

    More space?

    Doesn't help a frightened dog. Panic isn't about square footage. It does help a bored one, and it helps a dog with confinement distress specifically.

    Timeline

    Expect four to twelve weeks for a dog who currently panics. Progress is not linear. Bad days are data, not failure.

    Module 07

    Being Alone

    Two skills get tangled here and untangling them is most of the work. One is being in the crate. The other is being without you. They are trained separately.

    Protest, distress, or a diagnosable disorder

    Veterinary behaviorists have argued that "separation anxiety" is really an umbrella covering several distinct problems with different emotional drivers: fear of being alone, fear of something that happens while alone, frustration at being confined, boredom, and generalized anxiety among them.14 They look similar on the doorbell camera and they need different plans, which is one reason self-diagnosis goes wrong.

    Get the camera

    Any cheap indoor camera. Without footage you are guessing, and the two most common guesses (he's fine, he's destroying the place out of spite) are both usually wrong. Watch the first fifteen minutes: that's where the answer is.

    Building duration

    The whole game is staying under the point where he panics, every single time. Ten seconds. Then thirty. Then two minutes, then back to twenty seconds. Progress is measured in weeks and it is never a straight line, and every session that ends in panic costs you several good ones.

    • 01Leave casually and predictably. Sneaking out keeps him vigilant, which is the opposite of the goal.
    • 02Drain the departure cues. Pick up your keys and sit back down. Put on your shoes and make lunch. Open the door and close it. Twenty times a day, going nowhere. The cues stop predicting anything.
    • 03Vary the length wildly. Five seconds, four minutes, forty seconds, ten minutes. If every absence is longer than the last, he learns to brace.
    • 04Come home boring. Quiet entry, no greeting through the bars, let him out once he's settled rather than while he's frantic. A huge reunion makes the next departure worse by contrast.
    • 05Don't stack a hard absence on a hard day. Trigger stacking applies here too.

    You come home after 40 minutes. The frozen Kong is untouched. What does that tell you?

    Eating is the threshold test. An untouched Kong means the whole absence was spent above the line, so the next session should be much shorter, not longer. Note the number and cut it in half.

    Things that help around the edges

    • Sound masking. A fan, white noise machine, or low talk radio near the front door. For a dog who alerts to hallway people this is doing more than you'd think, and it also covers the sound of the elevator, which is often the actual trigger.
    • A draft stopper along the bottom of the door. Cuts sound and, just as importantly, scent. Half of what an apartment dog alerts to is smelling someone pass.
    • A midday break for the first months: a walker, a neighbor, a lunch trip. Duration training and eight-hour absences cannot coexist; you can't teach him that absences are short while also doing long ones.
    • Adaptil and similar pheromone diffusers have mixed evidence. Cheap, harmless, not a plan.
    When to stop DIY-ing

    If he is injuring himself, destroying doorframes, or has not improved at all after four consistent weeks, that is a clinical presentation and it needs a veterinary behaviorist (DACVB) or a certified separation anxiety trainer, not more of the same protocol. Anti-anxiety medication frequently makes the behavioral work possible rather than replacing it.15

    Module 08

    The Leash, Part One: Pulling

    He pulls because pulling works. Every time he pulls and the world gets closer, the behavior gets paid. That is the entire mechanism and it is also the entire solution.

    Why it's hard to fix

    Three things are stacked against you. First, a dog's comfortable walking pace is faster than yours, so the default state is him being ahead. Second, leash pressure is self-reinforcing: pressure builds, he leans into it, he gets closer to the thing, pressure paid off. Third, you cannot practice loose-leash walking during your actual walks while also getting anywhere, so the two goals fight.

    So split them. Training walks are short, boring, and go nowhere: ten minutes, one block, entirely about the leash. Getting-somewhere walks use management (a front-clip harness, a shorter route, a long line in a field) and you accept the pulling. Trying to train on every walk means training on none of them.

    Equipment, honestly

    The evidence here is thinner than the internet suggests, so here is what's actually known. A study comparing a martingale collar, a front-connection harness, a Starmark polymer collar and a metal prong collar found dogs pulled significantly harder on the plain martingale than on any of the other three, with no significant differences among those three, and the authors found no clear evidence of poorer welfare from equipment type in the behaviors they could measure.16 An earlier study comparing a collar to a harness found no significant behavioral difference in the parameters measured.17

    What that means: harnesses are not magic and prong collars are not uniquely effective. The reason to choose a well-fitted Y-front harness anyway is mechanical, not behavioral. A small dog who lunges into a flat collar is putting his whole force through his trachea and cervical spine, and the professional bodies are unambiguous that aversive equipment (prong, choke, e-collar) should not be used.12

    Y-front harness, front and back clips

    Straps that clear the shoulder joint, not a horizontal band across the chest. Front clip for redirecting a lunge, back clip for relaxed walks and long lines. This is the default.

    Six-foot leash for training

    Fixed length. Not retractable: retractables teach constant light pressure, which is exactly the sensation you're trying to make meaningful.

    Fifteen to thirty-foot long line

    For decompression walks in open space, and the only way he should be loose around people right now. Biothane, so it doesn't hold water or burn your hands. Attach to the back clip.

    Skip

    Prong, choke, slip leads, e-collars, and "no-pull" harnesses that tighten on the armpits. For a dog already wary of strangers, adding pain to the moment a stranger appears is the worst available move.

    The core skill: pressure means something

    Before any of the games, teach him that a taut leash is information rather than an obstacle. This takes about three sessions in your hallway.

    Stand still, apply the lightest possible pressure

    Two fingers of tension toward you. Do not pull. Just hold it and wait, up to thirty seconds.

    Mark any movement toward you

    A weight shift, a head turn, a step. The instant the leash goes even slightly slack: "yes," release all pressure, feed. The release of pressure is itself part of the payment.

    Repeat until he yields instantly

    Ten reps a session, three sessions. Then take it outside where it's harder and drop the difficulty back down.

    Ready when: light tension produces an immediate turn toward you

    The games that actually build a loose leash

    These come out of Leslie McDevitt's pattern-game work, which is built specifically for dogs who need predictability in order to think.1819 A pattern is a short repeating loop the dog can predict; predictability lowers arousal, and a dog with lowered arousal can walk.

    1-2-3 Treat

    Count out loud, walking. On "three," food appears at your side. That's it. Within a day he starts orienting to you on "two." It's a portable rhythm you can start anywhere, including mid-meltdown.

    Up-Down

    Drop food on the ground. He eats it, looks up at you, you mark and drop another. Builds a repeating loop of look-at-me that survives distraction, because eating off the floor is calming in itself.

    Magnet hand

    Food held at your left seam, he follows it for three steps, gets it. Then four steps. A crude lure at first; fade the food out of the hand within a week so the hand position alone becomes the cue.

    Silky leash

    Practise the pressure exercise above at walking speed. Feather-light tension, he yields, pressure vanishes, he's paid. Never escalate the pressure; if he doesn't respond, you were too close to something.

    Two techniques you'll read about, with caveats

    "Be a tree"

    Stop dead every time the leash tightens, move again when it slackens. It works, slowly, on a dog who is pulling out of enthusiasm. It does very little for a dog who is pulling out of fear or who is over threshold, and it makes walks so tedious that most people abandon it in week two. Use it as a habit, not as the plan.

    Penalty yards

    Walk backward a few steps every time he pulls. Same story: fine for enthusiasm, actively bad if the thing he's pulling toward is something he's afraid of, because now you're yanking a scared dog around.

    The realistic standard

    Do not aim for a heel. A heel is a competition behavior and it's miserable for both of you on a daily walk. Aim for: leash stays slack, he checks in occasionally, he can be redirected when you need it. Everything else is a bonus.

    Practically, tell him which mode you're in. A harness back clip and a long line means "go be a dog." The front clip and a short leash means "we're walking together." Dogs read equipment as context extremely fast, and having a clear signal for each is worth more than any single technique.

    He drags you toward every corner and lamppost for the first five minutes of every walk. Best first move?

    The first five minutes of a walk are the highest-arousal, highest-information part of a dog's day. Let him unload it. Asking a dog to do precision work at his peak arousal, before he's read the news, is asking for a fight you don't need to have. Fetch beforehand raises arousal rather than lowering it.

    Module 09

    The Leash, Part Two: When He Won't Move

    He plants, or he finds a smell and refuses to leave it. These look like the same problem and they are not. One is a feeling; one is a negotiation. Different fixes.

    The one absolute rule

    Never drag him. Dragging a planted dog does three things at once: it teaches him that the leash is something to brace against, it pairs your hands with an unpleasant sensation, and if he planted out of fear, it forces him toward the frightening thing while he's already over threshold. It also risks real injury to a small dog's neck. Wait, or change what you're asking.

    First, diagnose it

    He just stopped walking. Which is it?

    The fear stop

    Body low or stiff, ears back, weight shifted backward, staring at something specific, won't take food. This is not stubbornness, it's a dog telling you he can't. What to do:

    • 01Stop asking. Let the leash go slack and stand still. Silence. Give him ten to twenty seconds to look at the thing and process it.
    • 02Increase distance without going toward the trigger. Move sideways or backward, and let him follow. A dog who can move away regains the ability to think.
    • 03Offer food at nose level once you have distance. If he eats, you're back under threshold and can walk. If he won't, you need more distance.
    • 04Then go home or go around. Do not push through it. Pushing through builds the exact association you're trying to avoid.

    The sniff stop, and the single best tool for it

    He has found something and he is not leaving. This is not defiance, it's that sniffing is one of the most rewarding things a dog does. Nosework has been shown to shift dogs toward measurably more optimistic judgment in a cognitive bias test.20 It is, in the most literal sense, good for him.

    So don't fight it. Put it on cue and then use it as currency. This is the Premack principle and it's the highest-return trick in this entire module.

    Name the thing he already wants

    When he's already sniffing something, say "go sniff" in a warm voice. Do this for a week. The phrase attaches to permission.

    Buy walking with sniffing

    Take five steps of loose leash, then "go sniff!" and let him go to the nearest interesting thing. Walking now predicts sniffing rather than preventing it.

    This inverts the whole dynamic. He stops seeing you as the obstacle.

    Add a release from the sniff

    After 20–30 seconds, say "let's go" cheerfully, take one step, and pay him when he catches up. Never yank on "let's go" or the phrase becomes a threat.

    Ready when: he leaves a smell on the phrase, most times

    Extend the walking

    Five steps becomes ten becomes half a block. Keep the ratio generous; a dog who has to walk two blocks per sniff will stop believing you.

    Design the walk around this

    Give him one genuinely long decompression walk a week: long line, somewhere green and empty, no destination, his nose sets the pace for forty minutes. Then the daily walks can be shorter and more structured without him feeling starved of the thing he needs. A dog who never gets to sniff freely will fight you for it on every walk. This is also where the activity budget from Module 02 gets spent without spending exposure.

    The overwhelm stop

    Some stops are neither fear nor negotiation: he is simply flooded. Too much street, too many people, too much noise for a ten-month-old who lived somewhere else last month. Signs are scattered rather than pointed: he stops, starts, spins, can't commit, won't sniff either.

    The answer is not to build tolerance by staying out longer. It's to make the walk smaller. Ten minutes on a quiet street. The same route every day for two weeks, so the route stops being new information. Then add one variable at a time.

    Getting him moving again, mechanically

    Scatter and go

    Toss five pieces of food on the ground in the direction you want to travel. He eats his way there. Movement restarts without any pressure on the leash.

    Turn into a curve

    Don't walk directly at what stopped him. Arc wide around it. Curving is what dogs do naturally to defuse a tense approach, and it lowers the stakes.

    1-2-3 Treat

    Start counting. The pattern is familiar and portable, and it gives a stuck dog something predictable to do, which is often enough to unstick him.

    Change your body

    Turn side-on, crouch slightly, look away from him, take a step. Facing a dog head-on and staring is social pressure; turning away invites following.

    Halfway down the block he plants, stares at a doorway, and won't take chicken. You're two minutes from home. Best move?

    Refusing food is the threshold test failing. Distance is the only thing that fixes it, and the direction that matters is away from the trigger, not toward home. Carrying him is fine as a last resort in a genuine emergency, but it teaches him nothing and it removes his ability to move away himself.

    Module 10

    Strangers

    The hardest module and the most important one. It has been rewritten, because the first version of it was wrong about what he is doing.

    What changed, and why

    This module used to be called Men, Strangers and the Door, and it assumed a small dog frightened of large men, working on a leash. Closer observation says otherwise: there is no gender or age pattern, it happens off leash as much as on, and he moves toward people rather than away. The plan below is different in one important way from the old one, and the difference is the whole module.

    What he is actually doing

    Four observations, and together they identify it precisely.

    It stops when the person leaves

    Not when he calms down, not when he's reassured. Departure ends it. That makes it a distance-increasing display by definition, whatever it looks like on the way in.

    He is excellent with dogs

    This rules out general arousal, general fearfulness, and temperament. His social software is fine. Humans are the category that never got installed.

    No pattern in who

    Not men, not children, not size or voice. The trigger is not a feature of the person. It is that the person is unfamiliar.

    He habituates

    He barked at you when you first met and has never barked at you since. At your aunt's he barked, settled, then approached her and was friendly. The curve completes if it is allowed to.

    Put together: this is novelty alarm with a slow off-switch. His alarm system fires on unfamiliar humans, takes a long time to shut off, and re-fires when a known person does something new (starts talking, stands up, approaches the car). Once someone crosses into known, they are permanently fine.

    This is not fear of people, and the distinction is load-bearing

    A dog who was afraid of humans as a class would not approach one voluntarily and be friendly ten minutes later. What you have is a dog with a gap in his early experience of people, whose alarm fires on the unfamiliar and takes too long to come down. That is a far better problem to have, and it responds to a different plan: not primarily counterconditioning, but engineered habituation.

    Where the gap probably came from

    Wariness of unfamiliar people is one of the most common canine anxiety traits, not a rare or shameful one: in a survey of 13,700 Finnish pet dogs, general fearfulness affected around 29% of dogs, with fear of strangers among the most prevalent subtypes.21 A companion study found social fearfulness was associated with inadequate socialisation as a puppy, low activity, urban living, and being a first dog in the household.22

    His critical socialisation window, roughly 3 to 12 or 14 weeks, closed months before you met him. Whatever he did or didn't meet in that window is fixed now. It is also possible something specific happened. You will never know which, and it changes nothing about the plan, so stop investigating and start scheduling.

    Why "socialise him more" is exactly wrong for this dog

    It is the first advice anyone will give you and it will make him worse, for a specific mechanical reason.

    Habituation only completes if the same stimulus comes back. A person he meets once and never sees again cannot habituate; the curve gets started and abandoned. Worse, every one of those encounters ends the same way, with the person leaving while he is barking, which pays the barking on the most reliable schedule there is.

    The dog park is the worst place he could be right now

    Not because it is overwhelming. Because every human in it is someone he will never see again. He is running dozens of repetitions a week of bark until the novel human is gone, off leash, where you cannot interrupt it, and not one of those curves ever completes. If he needs dog time, use the neighbour's dog. Go at empty hours or not at all.

    The correct prescription is the inverse of the usual one: fewer humans, repeated. Not more humans, novel.

    The roster: the actual intervention

    Everything else in this module supports this one thing.

    Pick five or six people

    Friends, neighbours, family, your dog walker. People who can realistically show up repeatedly over a couple of months. Variety of age and sex is nice but it is not the point; repetition is the point.

    Each one converts from novel to known

    Six or so exposures over three weeks does it for most dogs. You already have proof of concept twice over: yourself, and most of the way through your aunt.

    Converted when: he notices them arrive and does not start

    The rule generalises before the people run out

    This is why it works beyond the six. After enough individual humans have gone from alarming to safe, the category itself starts to soften. You are not training six exceptions; you are teaching him that unfamiliar humans turn into familiar ones.

    Only then widen it

    New people, new places, busier times. One variable at a time, several days apart.

    Six names, and when each one can realistically come round. Then track the sessions.

    The rule that makes the rest possible

    He is on a long line around people. Not negotiable right now.

    Three reasons, and the first is the one that matters. You have no ability to control distance, and distance is the mechanism of every technique in this manual. Every off-leash rep is a full-strength reinforcement you cannot interrupt. And a dog who runs at people barking generates bite reports and citations with a closed mouth, and a startled person going down on pavement is your liability, two weeks into an adoption.

    A fifteen to thirty foot biothane long line on the back clip of a Y-harness gives him most of the freedom and gives you the dial. It is not a punishment and it is not forever.

    You produce the distance, before he barks

    This is the whole intervention in one line, and it is worth reading twice.

    Right now the sequence is: he notices, he barks, the person leaves, barking worked. You are going to replace it with: he notices, you move away immediately, quiet noticing worked.

    You are handing him the exact outcome he has been barking to produce, in exchange for noticing calmly. Distance is the reinforcer he actually wants, which is why food alone has been doing less than you'd expect.

    Look up BAT

    Grisha Stewart's Behavior Adjustment Training is the named protocol for exactly this: a dog whose reinforcer is distance rather than food.24 Long line, big open space, a helper at distance, and you follow the dog while he gathers information at his own pace and chooses to disengage; his own choice produces the distance. It is a better fit for him than food pairing alone, and it is worth a session with a trainer who already uses it to set up.

    Distance is still the dial

    Everything below only works below threshold, and distance is the only variable that reliably moves him below it. Before you train anything, find his current number.

    Find his working distance

    Drag to see roughly what happens at each distance for a typical dog at his stage. Your real number comes from watching him, not from this slider, but this is the shape of it.

    40 feet

    Food pairing, running underneath

    Counterconditioning is not the primary mechanism here, but it is worth running alongside, because it costs nothing and it speeds the association up. You are changing what a stranger predicts.

    A person appears at a distance where Earthwind notices but does not react

    He looks. Body stays loose. He can still eat. That distance is your starting line, and on a bad day it might be a hundred feet.

    The instant he sees them, food starts

    Not after he looks at you. Not as a reward for calm. The food is triggered by the person's appearance, like a switch. Feed continuously, piece after piece, while they are visible.

    Trigger first, food second. Always.

    They disappear. The food stops instantly.

    Dead silence, no food, nothing interesting. The contrast is the whole lesson: strangers are the only time the good stuff happens.

    Repeat until you see the head-whip

    After enough repetitions he'll see someone and immediately look at you with a happy expression. That's the conditioned emotional response flipping. It is the moment you're working toward and you'll know it when it happens.

    Only now do you decrease distance, by a little

    Shrink the distance slowly, and let it go backward

    Some days you'll need more room than yesterday. That's trigger stacking, not regression. Take the distance he needs today.

    The most common mistake

    Feeding to distract him before he notices. If the food comes first and the person second, you've taught him that food predicts strangers, which makes food mildly scary and does nothing for the stranger. Let him see them. Then pay.

    The second most common mistake

    Working too close because progress feels too slow. Every reaction over threshold is a rehearsal that undoes several good sessions. Boring, easy, far-away repetitions are the fast route. This will not feel like it.

    The visitor protocol

    This is the roster in practice, and it is written from what happened at your aunt's, where the habituation was working until it got interrupted. Half of this problem is other people, and their instinct to be friendly is the accelerant.

    The person is seated before he comes in

    Already sitting, already settled, side-on, not facing the door. A person who is stationary and boring when he first perceives them starts the curve much lower than one who walks in and greets him.

    They talk to you, continuously and boringly, from before he arrives

    This is the fix for what happened with your aunt. The onset of a voice is its own discrete trigger. If the talking is already happening and never stops, there is no onset to react to.

    The most-missed step on this page

    No eye contact, no reaching, no talking to him. At all.

    However nice they want to be. Turning to face him, leaning down, extending a hand and using a high voice is a stack of social pressure delivered to a dog who is already deciding. Tell them in advance, not in the moment.

    He is on a long line or behind a gate, at a distance where he can look away

    Scatter food on the floor for him. Sniffing drops arousal faster than anything else available to you in a living room.

    Let the curve run. Do not accelerate it.

    He will bark, then get bored, then find something else, then eventually approach. That whole sequence is the mechanism working. It takes as long as it takes.

    Ready for the next step when: he approaches voluntarily

    When he approaches and is friendly, the person still does nothing

    This is where it gets lost. The friendly approach feels like an invitation and it is not. No petting, no talking, no looking. He investigates a boring object and leaves. Petting and conversation come in later sessions, not later minutes.

    Pre-announce every change

    "I'm going to stand up now," said to you, while you feed him through it. Standing, moving toward him, raising a voice, leaving the room and coming back are all fresh events to his alarm system.

    Forty minutes into a visit, he has finally settled, walked over to your friend and sniffed her hand. She is delighted. What should she do?

    This is the exact moment the visit at your aunt's came apart. His approach is information-gathering, not consent to be handled. A person who stays boring at that moment gets to stay in the room; a person who reaches or speaks resets him and teaches him that approaching produces pressure. Hand-feeding by strangers is the last step of all, if ever, because the conflict of wanting the food and fearing the hand is exactly the state that produces bites.

    Scripts for the humans

    Have the sentences ready so you're not improvising while your dog is escalating. Note that these are the opposite of what most people expect to hear, and saying them warmly and early is what makes them land.

    • "Please ignore him and keep walking." The single most useful sentence you own. Said early, warmly, while moving.
    • "He's in training, please don't look at him." For someone who has already stopped.
    • "Please don't reach for him." Direct. Use it before the hand is moving, not after.
    • "He's nervous about new people, so we're going to walk past for a couple of weeks before he says hello." For a neighbour you'll see daily. This buys you enormous goodwill and converts a future complaint into a heads-up.
    • For a roster person, in advance and in writing: "Be sitting down before we come in, talk to me not him, no eye contact, and if he comes over just carry on talking and don't touch him."
    • A bright leash sleeve or vest that says NERVOUS or DO NOT PET does more work than any sentence.

    The car

    The car is its own problem and it deserves to be managed rather than trained for now. He is confined, he cannot move away, you are in there with him, and someone is approaching. That is four things at once.

    • Don't have people approach the car. Park further away and walk to them.
    • A crate or a covered crate in the car removes the visual entirely and is worth doing anyway for safety.
    • If someone does approach, drive or move rather than sitting there letting it run.
    • Come back to this in a couple of months, after the roster has done its work.

    The door

    The doorway is its own problem because it's the highest-arousal square metre in the apartment, and because every single knock has, so far, been followed by a stranger materialising inside his home.

    Manage it completely for two weeks

    Baby gate across the hall, or he goes to the bedroom before the door opens, every time. No exceptions, including for the delivery guy. You're stopping the rehearsals.

    Decouple the sound

    Knock on your own wall or play a doorbell recording at low volume. The instant it sounds, food appears. Nobody comes in. Twenty reps a day, several days. Then raise the volume.

    Ready when: the sound makes him look at you expectantly

    Build a station

    Teach a mat or bed six to ten feet from the door as the place where food happens (see Mat Work in the games library). Then add the sound. Sound, go to mat, get paid there.

    Ready when: the knock sends him to the mat on his own

    Rehearse with a roster person

    An accomplice knocks and leaves. Then knocks, comes in, ignores him completely and sits down. Then the full visitor protocol above. Pay throughout.

    Apartment specifics

    Blinds closed on any window facing a walkway. Crate and bed as far from the entry as the floor plan allows. Fan or white noise at the door. If you have a peephole camera, learn the delivery window and pre-empt it with a chew. Take the stairs at busy hours so you're not trapped in an elevator with a stranger at three feet.

    Games that make it an activity instead of an ordeal

    Look At That

    From Leslie McDevitt's Control Unleashed. Teach "look at that" as a cue meaning go ahead and look, then come back to me for payment. Turns the trigger into the first step of a game he knows how to win.18

    Engage–Disengage

    Stage one: mark and pay the instant he looks at the person. Stage two: wait for him to look away on his own, then mark and pay. Stage two builds a dog who checks in voluntarily.

    The U-turn

    Your emergency exit, and the thing that makes "you produce the distance" possible. Practise it on empty streets until it's automatic, so it's available when you need it.

    1-2-3 Treat

    Your emergency pattern. Start counting the moment someone turns the corner and keep walking. It gives him something predictable to do and gives you something to do with your hands and voice besides tightening the leash.

    Find It

    Toss food on the ground away from the trigger. Sniffing and eating are both incompatible with barking, and the head-down posture lowers arousal.

    Treat and Retreat

    For a seated roster person, later on. They toss food behind Earthwind, so approaching is always followed by moving away. Removes the trap of luring a wary dog too close to something he can't leave.23

    Recall, as a separate project

    He needs a recall before he can ever be loose again, and it must be built on nothing: an empty field, a brand new word, absurd payment, no people anywhere. Do not test it near a trigger until it is boring. See Ping Pong Recall in the games library. A recall that has failed once near a person is a recall you have to rename.

    Muzzle training, before you need it

    Train a basket muzzle now, while nothing has happened. A muzzle-trained dog can be safely handled at the vet, groomed, and walked past a surprise without risk, and the training itself is a pleasant food game. Dogs who are muzzled for the first time during an emergency learn that muzzles predict emergencies.25 It is a competence, not an admission, and for a dog who does not growl first it is a sensible piece of insurance.

    Timeline and expectations

    • Two weeks of reset first (Module 02), before any of this starts.
    • Three to four weeks per roster person to convert from novel to known.
    • Four to eight weeks for visible change in how he meets a new person, and it will be uneven.
    • Six months to a year for a genuinely relaxed dog, if you get there.
    • The honest target is neutrality, not friendliness. He can very likely become a dog who notices a stranger and keeps walking. He may never be a dog who wants to be petted by one. Aim at the first thing; the second is a bonus, not the goal.
    The prognosis, plainly

    It is good. He is ten months old, he is socially fluent with dogs, he approaches people voluntarily once his arousal drops, and he has already completed the habituation curve with at least one human. Nothing here is fixed. Months of work, not weeks, and the work is mostly boring.

    Teeth

    At ten months this is not teething. Adult teeth are in. Nipping now is about arousal, play, frustration, or a request, and each one has a different fix.

    Tell the difference

    Play mouthing comes with a loose wiggly body, a play bow, exaggerated movement, and pauses. Warning bites come with stillness, a hard stare, a closed tight mouth, a growl, or a freeze first. If you're seeing the second kind, this module is not your module: go to Module 15 and get a professional. Do not punish it and do not push through it.

    The mechanism, and why "yelp" often backfires

    The standard advice is to yelp when teeth touch skin, imitating a littermate. It works for some puppies. For an aroused adolescent it very often does the opposite: a high-pitched squeal is an excellent play signal and it makes the game more exciting. If yelping has made things worse, that's why.

    The reliable version is negative punishment: teeth touch skin, and the thing he wants (you, the game, the attention) instantly stops. Nothing unpleasant is added. He learns that his teeth end the fun.

    Decide the line in advance

    Any tooth contact with skin ends it. Not "hard" contact. A blurry rule teaches a dog to test where the line is today.

    Mark it neutrally and leave

    A flat "oops" or nothing at all, then stand up, fold your arms, turn your back, or step over a baby gate. Twenty to thirty seconds. No scolding, no eye contact, no drama; drama is attention.

    Come back and resume

    Calmly. The game continues. He needs the chance to do it correctly, or he's just learning that you disappear randomly.

    Repeat with total consistency

    Everyone in the household, every time, for two to three weeks. One person who lets him mouth keeps the behavior alive for everybody.

    Expect an extinction burst first. It gets worse before it stops.

    The part that actually fixes it: prevent, don't respond

    Responding well to a nip is damage control. The real work is upstream.

    • 01Have a toy in your hand before the interaction starts. A dog who is already holding something cannot be holding your wrist. Keep a tug rope in every room.
    • 02End play one minute before he'd end it. Track when the nipping usually starts, then stop three minutes earlier. Every session that ends calmly is a rep of calm; every session that ends in teeth is a rep of teeth.
    • 03Check the clock, not the toy box. A dog who has been awake and stimulated all afternoon looks manic rather than sleepy. Nipping that starts at 5pm daily is a nap problem.
    • 04Give the mouth a legal job. Chewing and licking lower arousal. A chew after play is a better intervention than any correction. Module 13 covers what he'll actually take.
    • 05Build an off switch. Play, pause, ask for a sit, resume. Repeatedly interrupting play and restarting it teaches him to come back down from excitement, which is the underlying skill.

    Tug, with rules, is the best possible outlet

    Tug is not aggressive and it does not make dogs dominant. It's a cooperative predatory game and it's an excellent way to teach a mouthy dog exactly where his teeth are allowed. Rules:

    • You start it, with a cue ("get it").
    • Teeth on skin, even accidentally: game stops for 20 seconds. Every time.
    • He must release on a cue ("drop"). Teach it by holding still and offering food; the moment he lets go, mark, feed, and immediately restart the game. The reward for dropping is more tug.
    • Let him win often. Winning is not a status event, it's what makes the game worth playing.
    • Two or three short bouts, then it goes away. The toy lives out of reach so it stays special.
    Bite inhibition

    Bite inhibition is a dog's learned control over jaw pressure, built mostly in puppyhood through play with other dogs. It's the single thing that determines whether a future startled dog leaves a bruise or a puncture. At ten months you can't build it from scratch, but you can maintain it: keep his safe, well-matched play with the downstairs neighbor's dog going, since that's where dogs practice calibrating their mouths.2627 For this dog that play session is doing three jobs at once: bite inhibition, the activity budget, and his social life, all without spending a single unit of the exposure budget.

    Every evening around six, play escalates and he starts grabbing sleeves. You've tried yelping and it made him wilder. Best plan?

    A predictable daily time means it's an arousal-and-fatigue pattern, not a training gap. Playing longer raises arousal; muzzle-holding adds an aversive to an over-threshold dog and risks making hands frightening. Change the schedule and the behavior often disappears without being trained at all.

    Module 12

    The Games Library

    Thirty games, each one a training protocol in disguise. Filter by what you're trying to fix, or by how much brain you have left today. Tap a card to open it.

    Two framing notes before you start. First, mental work tires a dog far more than physical work, and a tired-out athlete is just a fitter dog with more stamina for trouble. Ten minutes of nosework beats a half-hour march. Second, retire the food bowl. Every meal that comes out of a game instead of a dish is free training, free enrichment, and free relationship-building, at no cost in time you weren't already spending.

    Builds

    Arousal reduction, a portable emergency behavior, and confidence in new spaces.

    How
    1. Show him a piece of food, say "find it," and toss it a foot away.
    2. When that's easy, hide five pieces around one room while he waits in another.
    3. Release him with the cue. Let him work. Don't help.
    4. Scale up: harder hiding places, a whole apartment, then outdoors on a long line.
    Why it works

    Sniffing engages a huge amount of neural real estate and lowers heart rate. Nosework has been shown to shift dogs toward more optimistic judgments in cognitive bias testing, meaning the effect outlasts the game.20 It is also physically incompatible with barking.

    Builds

    Slower eating, ten to twenty minutes of foraging out of a ninety-second meal, and a fast way to bring a wound-up dog down.

    How
    1. Take his measured meal and scatter it across a towel, a snuffle mat, or a patch of grass.
    2. Walk away. Let him hunt.
    Why it works

    Foraging is a natural behavior with its own intrinsic reward. Head-down sniffing posture is also a self-calming position, which is why this works as an intervention as well as a meal.

    Builds

    Problem-solving, persistence, and a legal outlet for destruction. For a dog who ignores rubber and shreds paper, this is the single best match for what he actually wants.

    How
    1. Wrap kibble in crumpled paper. Put the parcels in a cardboard box with more crumpled paper.
    2. Give it to him. Supervise so he shreds rather than swallows.
    3. Make it progressively harder: taped flaps, boxes inside boxes, an egg carton.
    4. Rotate the container: cereal box, paper towel tube with the ends folded, a padded envelope.
    Why it works

    Costs nothing, satisfies the shredding urge on something you chose rather than your shoes, and builds the willingness to keep working at a problem, which transfers to training.

    Builds

    Persistence and paw-nose coordination. A ten-minute activity from things you own.

    How
    1. Drop food into the cups of a muffin tin.
    2. Cover each cup with a tennis ball or a rolled sock.
    3. Let him work them out. Start with only half the cups covered.
    Why it works

    A free version of a commercial puzzle feeder, and easy to make harder as he gets better at it.

    Builds

    Impulse control, nose-based decision making, and a two-minute game you can play anywhere including a vet waiting room.

    How
    1. Hide food in one closed fist, offer both.
    2. He sniffs and indicates one. Open it. If he's right, he gets it; if not, show him the empty hand and try again.
    3. Never let him mouth or paw at your hands; wait for a nose touch.
    Why it works

    Rewards deliberate information-gathering over grabbing, which is exactly the habit you want in a mouthy adolescent.

    Builds

    Forward movement past distractions, and a predictable pattern a frightened dog can hold onto. Probably the single most useful game here for Earthwind.

    How
    1. Walking, count out loud: "one, two, three." On "three," deliver food at your side.
    2. Repeat continuously. Keep the rhythm identical every time.
    3. Once he anticipates "three," start using it when something mildly interesting appears.
    4. Then use it as a person comes around the corner.
    Why it works

    From Leslie McDevitt's pattern games.1819 A predictable pattern gives an aroused dog something known to do, which lowers arousal enough for him to keep functioning. It also keeps you moving, which stops you from freezing and tightening the leash.

    Builds

    A fast, cheerful exit on cue. This is the mechanical skill that makes "you produce the distance" possible, and without it the whole of Module 10 stays theoretical.

    How
    1. On an empty street, in a bright voice, say your phrase ("this way!") and turn 180 degrees as you say it.
    2. Pay him the moment he is beside you facing the new direction. Generously, three or four pieces.
    3. Twenty reps a day for a week, with nothing happening. It must be a happy pattern, not a warning.
    4. Only then use it when you spot someone at a distance, well before he fires.
    Why it works

    Practised cold, it becomes automatic for both of you, so you are not improvising a decision while a stranger closes. Practised only in emergencies, the phrase becomes a predictor of stress and he starts to brace when he hears it.

    Builds

    Voluntary check-ins, and a loop of look-at-me that survives distraction.

    How
    1. Drop a piece of food at your feet.
    2. He eats it and looks up at you. That look is the behavior.
    3. Mark it and drop another. Repeat.
    4. Do this while a mild distraction is at a distance.
    Why it works

    You never ask for anything, so it can't fail, which makes it usable when a dog is too aroused to take cues. Eating off the ground is calming; looking up is the check-in you actually want.

    Builds

    An instant head-turn to his name, which is the foundation of every recall and every emergency redirect.

    How
    1. Say "Earthwind" once, in a neutral voice, when he isn't looking at you.
    2. The instant his head turns, mark and pay generously. Three pieces, not one.
    3. Never repeat the name. If he doesn't turn, you were too far, too loud, or he was too aroused. Get easier.
    4. Practise ten times a day for a month. Never use his name for anything unpleasant.
    Why it works

    Most dogs learn their name means nothing in particular because it's used constantly. Paid every time, it becomes the most reliable cue you own.

    Builds

    Turns "there is a stranger" from an alarm into the opening move of a game he knows how to win.

    How
    1. Start with a boring object at a distance: a trash can, a parked bike.
    2. He looks at it. Mark and pay. Repeat until he looks at it deliberately to earn the mark.
    3. Add the cue "look at that" just before he looks.
    4. Only now transfer it to real triggers, at a distance where he's comfortable.
    Why it works

    From Control Unleashed.18 It gives the dog permission to look, which removes the conflict of being told to ignore something he needs to monitor. Dogs who are allowed to look tend to look less.

    Builds

    Voluntary disengagement from a trigger, which is the skill that eventually replaces the barking.

    How
    1. Stage one: the instant he notices the trigger, mark and feed. Every time. For days.
    2. Stage two: once he's reliably turning back to you after the mark, stop marking the look. Wait. The moment he looks away from the trigger on his own, mark and jackpot.
    3. If he can't disengage within a few seconds, you're too close. Move.
    Why it works

    Stage one builds the positive association; stage two hands him the choice. The transfer of control is what makes it stick outside of training sessions.

    Builds

    Safe, voluntary approach to a stationary person, without trapping him in proximity.

    How
    1. The person sits, turned side-on, no eye contact, no talking to the dog.
    2. They toss a piece of food past Earthwind, behind him.
    3. He moves away to get it, then chooses whether to come back. Every approach is followed by a retreat.
    4. Over sessions, occasionally toss one slightly closer to the person. Never lure him past his comfort.
    Why it works

    Developed by Suzanne Clothier and widely used for wary dogs.23 The retreat is the active ingredient: it guarantees the dog never gets stuck close to something he's unsure about, which is where bites happen. Save it for roster people, not strangers.

    Builds

    A portable safe place: the dog stands between your feet, facing forward, with your legs as a physical shield.

    How
    1. Stand with feet apart. Lure him through from behind with food.
    2. Feed while he's in position. Release, repeat.
    3. Add the cue "middle." Build duration to twenty seconds.
    4. Use it when a cyclist passes or someone approaches on a narrow sidewalk.
    Why it works

    Gives a small nervous dog a defensible position that he chose, instead of being scooped up, which removes his agency and often escalates the panic.

    Builds

    A way to move him anywhere without touching him. Underrated. It is a recall, a leash-free redirect, and the basis of vet handling.

    How
    1. Hold a flat palm two inches from his nose. He investigates. The instant his nose touches, mark and pay.
    2. Repeat until deliberate, then add "touch."
    3. Move the hand: to the side, lower, higher, two steps away, so he follows it.
    4. Use it to move him off the couch, out of the doorway, into the crate, onto the scale at the vet.
    Why it works

    Replaces grabbing a collar, which is the single most common way owners accidentally teach a dog to avoid them.

    Builds

    A stationary, consenting position for ear cleaning, eye drops, brushing his face, and vet exams.

    How
    1. Hold your open palm flat, low. Lure his chin into it with food held under your hand.
    2. Mark the moment his chin makes contact. Build to five seconds, then twenty.
    3. Once solid, add handling: touch an ear while his chin is resting. If he lifts his head, stop immediately.
    Why it works

    Lifting his head is a clear, easy way for him to say stop, and honoring it every time is what makes him willing to stay. A dog who has a reliable "no" needs it far less often.

    Builds

    Explicit consent for grooming and handling. For a poodle facing a lifetime of clipping and combing, this is a major quality-of-life investment.

    How
    1. Put a clear bucket of food where he can see it, at his eye level, off to the side.
    2. Teach that looking at the bucket earns a piece from it. Looking at the bucket is now "I'm ready."
    3. While he's looking at the bucket, touch a paw briefly, then pay from the bucket.
    4. If he looks away, everything stops. That's his "not yet." Wait for him to look back.
    5. Build up: paw, brush stroke, ear, nail clipper touching a nail.
    Why it works

    Developed by Chirag Patel.2829 The dog controls the pace, which converts restraint into cooperation. Dogs who can stop a procedure reliably tend to tolerate far more of it.

    Builds

    A dog who happily shoves his face into a basket muzzle. Insurance for vet visits, grooming, and anything unexpected on the street.

    How
    1. Hold the basket muzzle, smear something good inside the end. He licks it out. Take it away.
    2. Repeat until he pushes his nose in eagerly. Do not fasten anything yet.
    3. Build duration with his nose inside, then touch the strap, then buckle for one second, then two.
    4. Muzzle on for good things: a walk, dinner, a game. Never only for vet visits.
    Why it works

    Muzzle conditioning is a normal, non-shameful competence, and doing it before an emergency is what makes it usable in one.25 Get a basket muzzle he can pant and drink in, not a fabric sleeve.

    Builds

    A portable off switch. Doorbell rings, he goes to the mat. Dinner is happening, he goes to the mat. Takes two weeks and pays out forever.

    How
    1. Put a towel or small mat down. Mark and pay any interaction with it: a look, a paw, standing on it.
    2. Pay only for four feet on. Then only for sitting. Then only for lying down.
    3. Feed on the mat, always, so the mat is where good things happen.
    4. Build duration, then distance, then distraction. Add the cue last.
    5. Move the mat around the apartment so it isn't a location, it's an object.
    Why it works

    Shaping rather than luring, so he figures it out himself, which produces a much more durable behavior. Also gives him a defined job during the chaotic moments that currently produce barking.

    Builds

    Actual physiological relaxation on a mat, under progressively harder conditions. A fifteen-day scripted program.

    How
    1. Download Karen Overall's Protocol for Relaxation.30 Each day is a written list of tasks.
    2. Day one: he holds a down while you take one step back, count to three, clap once, and so on.
    3. By day fifteen you're leaving the room, ringing the doorbell, and dropping things.
    4. Pay calmness, not compliance. If he's stiff and watchful, you're going too fast; repeat the day.
    Why it works

    It systematically desensitises a dog to the small disturbances of household life while he's in a relaxed posture, so relaxation itself becomes the trained behavior rather than obedience. Widely used in veterinary behavior practice as a foundation for anxiety cases.

    Builds

    More calm, by paying for it. Almost nobody does this and it changes dogs.

    How
    1. Keep a jar of food on a shelf in the main room.
    2. Any time you notice him lying down quietly on his own, walk over and calmly place one piece of food between his paws. Say nothing. Walk away.
    3. Do this ten times a day. Do not make it exciting; the goal is to reinforce the state, not interrupt it.
    Why it works

    Owners reliably reinforce noisy behavior with attention and ignore quiet behavior entirely. This inverts it. Reinforced states increase like any other behavior.

    Builds

    Impulse control as a general capacity, not a specific trick. The dog learns that backing off gets him the thing.

    How
    1. Hold food in a closed fist near him. He'll paw, mouth, nudge. Say and do nothing.
    2. The instant he backs off or stops, mark and open your hand to feed from the other hand.
    3. Progress to an open flat palm, then food on the floor under your hand, then uncovered food on the floor.
    4. Never let him self-serve. If he lunges, the hand closes.
    Why it works

    Popularised by Susan Garrett. It teaches restraint by making restraint the thing that pays, without any correction. Generalises unusually well to real situations: dropped food, an open door, a passing dog.

    Builds

    A dog who brings you things instead of running with them, and insurance against resource guarding.

    How
    1. He has a boring toy. Show him something better, say "drop," and swap. Give the toy back.
    2. Repeat many times a day with low-value objects. Always give it back.
    3. Work up to more valuable objects. Never take without paying.
    4. When he steals something real: walk calmly to the treat jar, don't look at him, and trade.
    Why it works

    Chasing a dog who has stolen something teaches him that stealing starts an excellent game and that human approach means losing things, which is how guarding is built. Trading teaches the opposite.

    Builds

    Physical outlet, a legal target for teeth, and impulse control if you build the rules in.

    How
    1. You start it with a cue. Long toy, so his teeth are far from your hands.
    2. Pull side to side, not up and down, to protect his neck and spine.
    3. Teeth on skin ends it, twenty seconds, every time.
    4. Ask for "drop," then immediately restart. The reward for dropping is more tug.
    5. Let him win frequently. Two or three short rounds, then the toy disappears.
    Why it works

    Tug does not create aggression or "dominance." What it does create is a structured, rule-bound way for a mouthy adolescent to use his mouth hard, with you, on something that isn't you.

    Builds

    Serious physical exercise in a small apartment or a small yard, plus practise at switching arousal off. Spends the activity budget with no exposure cost at all.

    How
    1. A pole with a rope and a lure. Drag it along the ground in wide circles, never yanking it upward.
    2. Let him catch it regularly. A chase he can never win is frustrating rather than satisfying.
    3. Ask for a sit or a down every thirty seconds, then restart. That interruption is the point.
    4. Two to three minutes, then done. This is high-intensity work.
    Why it works

    Satisfies the chase part of the predatory sequence, which is otherwise aimed at ankles and bicycles. The stop-start structure is where the off-switch gets built. Don't run it in the last hour before bed.

    Builds

    A retrieve that comes back, for the dog who fetches once and then keeps the toy.

    How
    1. Two identical toys. Throw one.
    2. When he picks it up, make the second one interesting: move it, squeak it.
    3. He drops the first and returns. Throw the second as he arrives.
    4. The return is what gets paid, not the pickup.
    Why it works

    Removes the conflict of having to give something up before getting the next throw. Fixes most "won't bring it back" problems in a session.

    Builds

    The ability to come down from excitement, which is the actual underlying skill behind nipping, barking, and lunging.

    How
    1. Play something exciting for fifteen seconds: tug, chase, flirt pole.
    2. Stop dead. Stand still, hold the toy against your body, say nothing.
    3. Wait. The instant he offers anything calm (four feet on the floor, a sit, a breath), mark and restart the game.
    4. Build up the length of the pause. Then the intensity of the play before it.
    Why it works

    The reward for calming down is the game restarting, which is the only reward a wound-up dog cares about. Practising the transition repeatedly is what builds the recovery, and food alone can't teach this because the dog doesn't want food at that moment.

    Builds

    A dog who runs into the crate because it's a game, not because he was told to.

    How
    1. Door propped fully open or removed.
    2. Toss a treat in. He goes in, eats, comes back out. Nothing happens.
    3. Toss another. Twenty repetitions. Never close the door during this game, ever.
    4. Once he's diving in, name it as he commits.
    Why it works

    Leaving is always permitted, which removes the trap. A dog who has never been shut in unexpectedly during this game keeps volunteering. See Module 06 for the full ladder.

    Builds

    Decompression. Not a training exercise; a welfare one, and it makes every training exercise work better. For this dog it is also how the activity budget gets spent without spending exposure.

    How
    1. Long line, back clip of the harness, somewhere quiet and green and genuinely empty.
    2. You go where he goes. No cues, no direction, no destination.
    3. Forty minutes. As often as you can manage, and at least twice a week.
    Why it works

    Sustained free sniffing shifts dogs toward measurably more optimistic judgments.20 A dog who gets this regularly fights you far less about sniffing on ordinary walks, because the need is already met. Length is not the constraint here; who else is there is the constraint.

    Builds

    A recall that's fun rather than obedient, built indoors where it can't fail. He needs this before he is ever loose in public again.

    How
    1. Two people, opposite ends of a hallway, food each.
    2. One calls him, pays lavishly when he arrives. Then the other calls.
    3. Ten rounds. Then take it outside on a long line, somewhere empty.
    4. Use a brand new word ("here!") rather than a word that has ever failed.
    Why it works

    Builds huge reinforcement history for the recall word in a context where he can't get it wrong. Never call him for anything unpleasant, or the word is spent. Do not test it near a person until it is boring everywhere else.

    Builds

    Tolerance for the grooming a poodle needs for the rest of his life. Start now, before it's urgent.

    How
    1. Touch one paw for one second. Mark and feed. That's the whole session.
    2. Over days: hold the paw, spread the toes, touch a nail, touch a nail with the clipper, clip one nail.
    3. Same for ears, muzzle, tail, armpits. One body part per session.
    4. Run the dryer or the clippers across the room during meals for a week before you ever point one at him.
    Why it works

    Dogs who were never taught to tolerate handling become adults who need sedation to be groomed. Ten seconds a day now prevents that entirely.

    How to actually use this page

    Pick three games this week. Not fifteen. One from Calm, one from Reactivity, one from Brain. Run them daily for a week, then swap one out. Consistency on three beats dabbling in thirty, and you'll actually remember the mechanics.

    Module 13

    The Basics Nobody Wrote Down

    Everything the rest of this manual quietly assumed you already knew. What actually goes in a Kong, where he sleeps, how often he needs to pee, how much to feed him, and what to buy.

    Potty: how many times, and when

    At ten months he is physically capable of holding it a long time. That is not the same as him needing to. Frequency is driven by intake and by events, not by the clock.

    WhenWhy
    Immediately on waking, morning and after every napA sleeping bladder empties on waking. This is the single most predictable one.
    10 to 30 minutes after every mealThe gastrocolic reflex. This is when most poops happen and it is very schedulable.
    After play, training, or anything excitingArousal empties bladders. The post-play accident is the classic one.
    Last thing before bedBoring, brief, no play. Just out and back.
    Any time he circles, sniffs the floor intently, or suddenly leaves the roomThose are the signals. Learn his, they are individual.

    Five to six trips a day is the normal shape at his age, most of them short. Only two of those need to be walks. The rest are ninety seconds outside for the specific purpose.

    How long can he hold it

    Roughly one hour per month of age plus one, capped at about eight. At ten months that is genuinely seven or eight hours overnight, and four to five is a much better daytime ceiling. Holding it is not the same as being fine.

    Overnight

    Most ten-month-olds sleep through. If he wakes you, take him out silently, no talking, no play, no treats, straight back to bed. Make it the most boring event of his day or you have built a 3am ritual.

    Pay it, every time, outside

    Three pieces of food the instant he finishes, outside, while he is still standing there. Not when he comes back in. Most house-training problems are timing problems.

    Accidents

    Say nothing, ever. Clean with an enzymatic cleaner, not a general one, or the scent marks the spot as a toilet. Punishment teaches dogs to go where you can't see, which is much worse.

    A quiet potty trip is a management decision

    For this dog, when you go out matters as much as how often. Take the last trip and the first trip at the times fewest people are around. Starting and ending the day with a reaction sets his baseline for the whole day, and it costs nothing to shift it by twenty minutes.

    Where he sleeps tonight

    Short answer: in your room, not in a shut crate, until the crate is rebuilt.

    He currently panics in the crate, and every panicked night is a repetition that makes Module 06 longer. He also cannot yet cope with not being able to see you. Those two facts settle it: he sleeps beside your bed, on a bed or in a pen or an open crate with the door off, where he can see you. That is not spoiling him and it is not a habit you'll be stuck with. It is the same logic as everything else in this manual: don't rehearse the panic.

    Tonight

    A bed on the floor next to yours, or a pen beside the bed, or the crate with its door removed. He can see you. Last potty, lights out, no fuss.

    Weeks two to eight

    Run the crate ladder in Module 06 during the day, in the living room, entirely separately from bedtime. Do not test it at night.

    Later

    Once he is happily latched in for twenty minutes with a Kong while you are in the room, move the crate next to your bed and close the door at night. You are within arm's reach, which makes night the easiest possible version of the task.

    Ready when: he settles latched, in the room, with you there

    Optional forever

    Plenty of well-adjusted dogs never sleep in a crate. If he ends up on a bed in your room permanently, that is a completely legitimate outcome. Keep the crate skill alive for vets and travel by feeding him in it once a week.

    What to do with him when you leave for work

    The honest answer

    Right now, a full workday alone is not available to him. He panics in confinement, he cannot yet be alone, and he is two weeks into a new home. This is a logistics problem to solve for the next two or three months, not a training problem you can train fast enough to avoid it.

    OptionVerdict for this dog
    Dog-proofed room behind a baby gate, camera on, Kong and chewBest default while the crate is being rebuilt. More space genuinely helps confinement distress even though it doesn't help panic.
    Exercise pen in the room you're usually inSecond best. He is already fighting the pen, so pair it with food and build duration the same way as the crate.
    Midday dog walker or a neighbourDo this regardless of the rest. Four to five hours is the ceiling, so a midday break is what makes a workday legal at all.
    DaycareCareful. Good for dog play, bad for him specifically: a room of unfamiliar humans all day is the dog park problem indoors. If you use one, pick a small one with consistent staff, so the humans become roster people.
    Crate, latched, all dayNo. Not yet, and possibly not ever for a full day.

    Whatever you pick, put a camera on it for the first week. You cannot plan around a behavior you have not seen, and the first fifteen minutes contain the answer.

    The Kong: what actually goes in it

    A Kong is not a chew toy. It is a food container, and an empty one is a boring piece of rubber that any sensible dog ignores. Nothing is wrong with him for ignoring it. Load it.

    The difficulty ladder

    Most people start on hard mode, the dog fails once, and writes the object off. Reverse that: the first three should be almost free.

    Smear the rim

    A little something soft around the inside of the opening. He licks it clean in thirty seconds. Do this twice and take it away while he still wants it.

    Loose fill

    Dry kibble, not packed. It falls out when he noses it. He learns the thing dispenses.

    Packed, wet

    Kibble soaked in warm water or low-sodium broth until soft, pressed in firmly. Ten to fifteen minutes of work.

    Packed and frozen

    Same, then freeze for at least four hours. Twenty to forty minutes. Freezing is the difficulty dial and it should be the last thing you add, not the first.

    This is the version that goes in the crate

    Plugged

    Frozen, with something larger jammed in the opening. Only once he is confident, and only if you want it to last an hour.

    Fillings, in order of usefulness

    His own food, soaked

    The base for almost everything. Kibble plus warm water or unsalted broth, left ten minutes, then packed. Costs no extra calories because it is his meal.

    Wet dog food

    Straight in, freezes beautifully. Use his own if he eats wet, or a can of a plain one as a topper.

    Plain canned pumpkin

    Pure pumpkin, not pie filling, which has sugar and spices. Cheap, low calorie, good for the gut, freezes well.

    Plain unsweetened yogurt

    Or kefir. A spoonful, if dairy sits well with him. Introduce one new thing at a time so you know what caused what.

    Peanut butter

    Small amounts, and read the label every single time. See the warning below. It is calorie dense, so a teaspoon, not a scoop.

    Mashed banana, cooked sweet potato, blueberries

    All fine, all cheap, all freeze well. Good for layering texture so it doesn't come out in one lump.

    The layering trick: something hard to reach at the closed end (a smear of the soft stuff), his packed soaked kibble in the middle, and something excellent visible at the opening. He gets an immediate win, then has to work, then gets a reason to keep going.

    Xylitol will kill a dog, and it is in peanut butter

    Xylitol, also labelled birch sugar, causes a catastrophic drop in blood sugar in dogs and can cause liver failure. It appears in some peanut butters, most sugar-free gum, some yogurts, and some baked goods. Read the ingredients on the jar you own, not the brand you remember.38 Also never: grapes and raisins, onion and garlic, chocolate, macadamia nuts, alcohol, and cooked bones of any kind, which splinter.

    If he still won't take a rubber Kong

    Drop it. Licking does the same parasympathetic job as chewing and takes less commitment. Use a lick mat: smear it, freeze it, suction it to the wall of the bath or the floor. A silicone baking mat works. So does the side of a bowl. You lose nothing by skipping rubber entirely, and he has already told you what he likes: things that shred. Cardboard, in Module 12, is the better match for him.

    Chews: what's safe, what breaks teeth

    He is a committed chewer, which is good news, and the concern is less about calories than teeth.

    The thumbnail rule

    If you cannot dent it with a thumbnail, or you would not want it dropped on your knee, it can fracture a molar. Slab fractures of the upper carnassial are among the most common dental findings in dogs and they run into four figures to fix. This rules out the most popular long-lasting chews, which is exactly why the fracture is so common.

    Fish skin twistsLow calorie, good for coat, gone fast.
    Bully sticksDigestible and popular, but calorie dense. Treat one as a meal replacement rather than an addition.
    Cardboard, egg cartons, paper towel tubesFree, matches what he already chooses, supervised so he shreds rather than eats.
    Rubber chews (Kong, Goughnuts, West Paw)Zero calories, always available. He may ignore them. That's fine.

    Supervised means in the room with your eyes on him, and taking it away when it gets small enough to swallow whole. Keep the stub, or trade it for something better rather than pulling it out of his mouth.

    Feeding: how much and how often

    Twice a day

    Morning and evening at roughly the same times. Predictability does real work for an anxious dog, and it makes the potty schedule predictable too.

    Measure it

    The bag's guidance is a starting point, not an instruction. Adjust by body condition, not by the number: ribs easily felt but not seen, a waist visible from above, a tuck from the side.39

    None of it from a bowl

    Scattered, in a Kong, in a snuffle mat, in a cardboard box, or paid out during training. Same food, same time cost, enormously more value.

    Treats under 10%

    Everything else, chews included, comes out of the daily total. An adolescent who gains weight fast is an adolescent whose joints pay for it later.

    Grazing, and the trap of topping the bowl

    Poodles are frequently unenthusiastic, slow, grazing eaters, and an adult will sometimes skip a day entirely. That is not a verdict on the food. The trap is what people do next — add something tasty on top, he eats it, and you conclude he was holding out. Now you own a dog who waits for the topper, and you have turned a non-problem into a daily negotiation. Feed it plain, take it up after a while, and let him be hungry for the next meal. A dog who is genuinely off his food for more than a day or two, or who is off his food and off himself, is a vet call rather than a menu problem.

    Changing foods is fine, and probably good. Rotating proteins and brands every few months is well tolerated and may reduce the odds of him developing a sensitivity to any one protein. Transition over three to ten days; if his stomach objects, slow the transition down and add plain canned pumpkin (the pure stuff, not pie filling — the same tin you are already using for his Kongs).

    The treat value ladder

    Have three tiers and do not mix them up. Using your best food for easy things leaves you nothing for the hard ones.

    TierWhatUsed for
    EverydayHis own kibbleKnown cues at home, capturing calm, scatter feeding
    GoodSoft commercial training treats, torn smallNew skills, training sessions, mildly distracting places
    ReservedChicken, cheese, hot dog, whatever he loses his mind forStrangers only. Nothing else. That is what makes the pairing in Module 10 work.

    Training pieces should be pea-sized and soft. Hard treats take too long to chew and break the rhythm; big treats fill him up in twenty reps. You want fifty deliveries out of a small handful.

    When you are buying the middle tier, the label filter is short: skip anything listing unnamed meat (“animal” anything), by-product, corn, wheat, soy, or artificial colors. That rules out most of the supermarket shelf and costs you nothing, because the tier that actually matters for Module 10 is chicken and cheese out of your own fridge.

    What to buy

    Essential now

    Y-front harness with front and back clips. Six-foot fixed leash. Fifteen to thirty-foot biothane long line. Enzymatic cleaner. Treat pouch that clips to your waistband.

    Essential now, for the work

    Indoor camera. Baby gate. Exercise pen. Lick mat and a Kong or Toppl. Snuffle mat, or a towel you roll food into.

    Soon

    Basket muzzle he can pant and drink in, for the game in Module 12. White noise machine or a fan for the door. Draft stopper. A leash sleeve that says DO NOT PET.

    Grooming

    Slicker brush and a metal greyhound comb. The comb is the one that matters: brushing the top does nothing if the mat is at the skin. A dematting comb and a finer detail comb for the face earn their keep too.

    Do not buy: retractable leashes, prong or choke or slip collars, e-collars, "no-pull" harnesses that tighten under the armpits, or a bark collar of any description.

    Grooming, because it is about to become urgent

    A poodle's puppy coat sheds out underneath the incoming adult coat somewhere between eight and fourteen months, and it mats spectacularly while it does — invisibly, right at the skin, while the surface still looks fine. This is the single most common reason young poodles get shaved to the skin at the groomer. He is in the middle of it now.

    • Comb to the skin, in sections, most days. Ten minutes. Line-comb: part the coat, comb the exposed layer from the roots out, move up an inch.
    • Behind the ears, armpits, collar line and the back of the thighs mat first. Check those even on a lazy day. Add the harness straps to that list — anywhere webbing sits against coat all day is a friction point, which is the one real argument for a collar over a harness. It is not a good enough reason to put a lunging twelve-pound dog back on a flat collar; just brush under the straps.
    • Pair every session with food from the start, and run the handling game in Module 12 alongside. A dog who fights grooming at ten months is a dog who needs sedation at four.
    • Nails every two weeks, and if you would rather not do them, a vet tech or groomer will for a few dollars. Long nails change how a dog loads his feet and stand, so this is structural, not cosmetic.

    Choosing a groomer, for this dog specifically

    A poodle coat is a professional's job; almost nobody keeps one right at home indefinitely. But for a dog who barks at unfamiliar people, which groomer and how you start matter more than the haircut. Ask on the phone, before booking:

    • 01"How do you handle anxious or barking dogs?" The answer you want involves patience and space, not getting through it.
    • 02"Is there a separate room?" A dog who can hear another dog panicking in a crate is a dog marinating in someone else's alarm for two hours.
    • 03"Will you do a slow-start plan?" Meet-and-greet first, then a bath and brush, then face, feet and sanitary, building to a full groom. Many shops have a package for exactly this.
    • 04Say plainly that you do not want him pushed through anything that frightens him, and that you would rather come back than finish the cut. Say it at booking, not at pickup.
    • 05Tip well and keep the same groomer. Every visit with a person he already knows is a roster session (Module 10) that happens to also cut his hair.

    Cadence. While he is still learning the place, every three to four weeks for a bath, brush-out, face, feet and sanitary trim. Once he has had a full groom and is comfortable, every six to eight weeks. The mistake is waiting until he is a shaggy mop and the first visit has to be a long one.

    If you bathe him at home, you must dry him

    A wet curly coat left to air dry tightens and pulls the coat into itself, and mats overnight. Bathing without a force dryer and a thorough brush-out afterwards makes the coat worse than not bathing him. Budget for a dog blow dryer if home baths are the plan, use a decent shampoo (cheap shampoo strips the coat and leaves the skin itchy), and see Volume One for the full bathing order.

    The vet, and two conversations worth having

    • Establish care now, before you need it, with a low-stress or Fear Free practice if you can find one. Take him in once just for treats and a weigh-in, with nothing done to him.
    • Rule out pain. Ask explicitly. A dog who hurts has a shorter fuse, and reactivity with a medical driver does not respond to behavior work. Worth doing before you spend three months on protocols.
    • Neutering timing is a real conversation and not a formality, and for him it is live now. Evidence on joint and cancer outcomes varies by breed and size, and for some breeds waiting until growth plates close is better.40 Neutering also does not reliably reduce fear-based reactivity, and in some studies is associated with more of it — so do not let anyone sell it to you as a fix for the barking. Ask your vet what the evidence says for his size and mix rather than defaulting to a date.
    • Make sure the microchip is registered to you, and that his tag has your current number. For a dog who bolts from strangers this is not paperwork, it is the plan.
    Neuter timing is live this month, and the answer is probably “not yet”

    He is about ten months old and around twelve pounds, which puts him right at the age a vet is likely to raise it — and right below the age most of the guidance says to wait for. Small-breed guidance commonly puts males under 30 lb at twelve months at the earliest, later if nothing about being intact is actually causing a problem, on the reasoning that hormones are still doing structural work while the growth plates close. The breed-and-size evidence points the same direction.40

    So if the surgery gets offered in the next couple of months, that is a conversation to slow down rather than a box to tick. Two things worth doing before you schedule: ask your vet to justify the timing for his size rather than by clinic default, and check whatever paperwork he came with — breeders and rescues often set a required window, and some tie a health guarantee to it.

    A cheap thing worth trying

    If his tags jingle constantly, get a tag silencer or a slide-on tag. For a dog whose whole problem is an alarm system that fires too easily, removing a noise he carries around with him costs a few dollars and might do nothing — but the downside is zero.

    It's a workday. He's been fine in a gated room for three hours and the frozen Kong is licked clean. What does that tell you?

    Same test in reverse. An untouched Kong means he was over threshold the whole time; a finished one means he could eat, which means he could think. Check the camera to see when he ate it, because a Kong finished in the first ten minutes and then four hours of pacing is a different story.

    Fill this in once and stop guessing. Update it monthly.

    Getting Help

    Dog training is an unregulated industry in the United States. Anyone can print a business card. Here is how to tell the difference, which matters more for a reactive dog than for almost any other case.

    Hire someone for this one

    Stranger-directed reactivity is the category where DIY most often goes wrong, and where a bad trainer does lasting damage. Budget for four to six sessions with someone qualified. This is the highest-value money in the first year. Ask specifically for someone who uses BAT or Control Unleashed, since that is the shape of the plan in Module 10.

    Credentials that mean something

    LettersWhat it isUse for
    DACVBVeterinary behaviorist. A veterinarian with a residency and board certification in behavior. The top of the field.31Severe fear, aggression, separation anxiety, anything that might need medication
    CAAB / ACAABCertified Applied Animal Behaviorist. PhD or Master's in behavioral science, certified by the Animal Behavior Society.32Complex behavior cases without a medical component
    CDBC / CBCC-KACertified behavior consultant, via IAABC or CCPDT. Assessed on behavior modification specifically, not just obedience.3334Reactivity, fear, resource guarding. Your most likely hire.
    CPDT-KA / KPA-CTPCertified professional trainer. Tested on learning theory and mechanics.3435Manners, crate, leash, foundation skills
    SAPT / CSATSeparation anxiety specialists, trained in that protocol specifically. Usually work remotely by video, which is ideal for this problem.15Separation anxiety, exclusively

    None of these are perfect and a certification is not a guarantee. But they mean someone was assessed by a third party, which is more than "10 years experience" tells you.

    Questions to ask before you book

    • 01"What happens when the dog gets it wrong?" The single most revealing question. You want to hear about lowering criteria, more distance, better rewards. If you hear about correction, consequences, or "letting him know it's not acceptable," that's your answer.
    • 02"What equipment do you use?" Prong, choke, slip lead, or e-collar in the answer means walk away, for this dog in particular.
    • 03"What's your certification, and who assessed you?"
    • 04"Can I watch a class or a session first?" Anyone who says no is telling you something.
    • 05"How would you handle a dog who runs at strangers barking, but is fine with dogs and settles after twenty minutes?" You now know enough from Module 10 to evaluate the answer. Distance, repeated exposure to the same people, and going at the dog's pace are the words you want. "He needs more socialisation, take him to the dog park" is the wrong answer.
    Red flags

    "Balanced training." "Pack leader," "alpha," "dominance," "he's testing you." Guaranteed results or a guaranteed timeline. Board-and-train where you can't watch. Any refusal to explain the method. Aversive tools framed as "just communication." A trainer who tells you your wary dog needs to be corrected out of it.

    Medication is not a failure

    For a dog whose baseline anxiety is high enough that he can't learn, behavioral medication (prescribed by a vet, ideally a DACVB) lowers the baseline enough for the training to work. It is not sedation and it is not a substitute for the protocols; it is what makes the protocols possible. Ask if you're four to eight weeks in with no movement.

    Where to start looking

    • Veterinary behaviorists: the ACVB directory. In your area, the UC Davis veterinary hospital runs a behavior service and takes referrals.3136
    • Behavior consultants: the IAABC and CCPDT directories, both searchable by zip.3334
    • BAT specifically: Grisha Stewart's site lists certified BAT instructors, and some work by video.24
    • Local: the SF SPCA and Berkeley Humane both run reward-based training programs and their behavior resources are free to read.27
    • Your vet first, regardless. Sudden or worsening reactivity sometimes has a medical driver: pain, thyroid, vision. A dog who hurts is a dog with a shorter fuse, and it is worth ruling out before you spend three months on behavior work.

    If he ever bites someone

    1. Separate immediately. Crate or another room. Do not punish him; it will not help and it makes the next one more likely to come without warning.
    2. Care for the person. Wash it, and get them medical attention. Be straightforward.
    3. Write down everything within the hour: who, where, what happened in the ten seconds before, body language, how many contacts, whether skin was broken and how deeply.
    4. Call your vet, and get a referral to a DACVB. A bite is a threshold event and it changes the plan.
    5. Know your local reporting requirements. Being the person who handled it properly is far better than being the person who didn't.

    On the strength of the evidence

    The welfare research on training methods is solid. The research on specific protocols (Look At That, BAT, Treat and Retreat, relaxation protocols) is thin: these are practitioner methods built on well-established learning principles, widely used and reported to work, but not tested in controlled trials the way the method-comparison studies have been. The roster approach in Module 10 is an application of habituation, which is one of the best-established phenomena in behavioral science, to a specific case; the general mechanism is not in doubt, but no trial has tested this particular protocol. This manual treats all of these as sensible applications of known mechanisms, not as proven interventions, and says so where it matters.
