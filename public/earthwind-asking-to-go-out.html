Teaching Him to Ask

Module 13 taught you to read the signals he already gives. This is the other direction: giving him one deliberate, unmistakable way to tell you he needs out — built for a ten-month-old who is anxious, noise-sensitive, and lives up a hallway.

The order matters, and it is not the order the internet gives you

Every bell-training article assumes a dog who wants to go outside. Yours is a dog for whom outside is the hard part.

A dog signals to make something good happen. If stepping into the hallway means footsteps, strangers, and the thing Module 10 is still working on, then the signal predicts something he would rather avoid — and a behaviour that predicts something unpleasant gets quieter, not louder. Teach the signal too early and you don't get a silent dog. You get a dog who learned that touching the bell summons the scary corridor, and now won't touch it at all. That is harder to undo than never having started.

Dogs also need to feel safe to eliminate at all. The muscles involved don't relax in a dog who is scanning.5 If he holds it outside and goes the moment he is back in, that is not defiance and not a house-training failure — it is the same fear, showing up as a full bladder.5

Gate

Don't start this until his ordinary potty trips are boring. Concretely: he walks out without freezing, empties within a few minutes on most trips, and you are not timing every departure around the corridor. If you are still running the reset in Module 02, or still shifting trips twenty minutes to dodge neighbours, finish that first. The signal is the last brick, not the first.

Meanwhile

Nothing here delays anything. Keep running Module 13's schedule — on waking, ten to thirty minutes after meals, after anything exciting, last thing at night. A signal never replaces the clock; it catches the trips the clock misses.

Step zero: write down what he already does

Module 13 names the usual tells — circling, sniffing the floor intently, suddenly leaving the room — and says his will be individual. Before you teach a new signal, spend a week learning his old one, because the whole trick of this training is catching him at the moment he is already asking.

Keep a note on your phone. Every time he goes out and actually produces something, write what he did in the sixty seconds before. Inside a week you will see two or three repeats. Those are your raw material.

Careful

A sudden change in how often he needs out is a vet question before it is a training question. New frequency, straining, accidents from a dog who had stopped having them, or drinking noticeably more — that is a urine sample, not a protocol.

Choosing the signal

The default advice is a strap of bells on the door handle. For most dogs it is fine. For this one it is the weakest of the four options, and for a reason that is already written into the rest of his manual: you are running white noise at night to mask hallway footsteps because sudden sharp sounds matter to him. A jangle of metal at nose height, produced by his own face, is a sudden sharp sound.

OptionHow it worksFor him
Come and get you You shape the contact he may already offer — come to you, nose your leg — into the ask. Best Silent. No hardware to be startled by. Builds on a behaviour he likely already has, and it strengthens the habit of bringing problems to you, which is the thing you want from a fearful dog generally.
Nose target on a mat or card A felt square or index card taped by the door at nose height. He touches it; you open. Good Silent, unambiguous, tied to the door. Needs you in the room to see it, which is the trade for the quiet.
Button A recordable button on the floor by the door, one word on it. Maybe Audible from another room, which is the advantage. Introduce the sound before the training, at volume, paired with food — not discovered by accident with his own paw.
Door bells The classic strap of bells, nudged with nose or paw.1 Last Loudest, most startling, most likely to be rung for reasons other than a bladder.3 If you use it anyway, muffle it first: tape over half the bells, or start with a single small one.
Pick one

One signal, one door, one meaning. Two channels means neither gets clear, and a dog with a reactive baseline does not need another ambiguity in his day.

The protocol

This is the standard three-phase shape — teach the behaviour, attach it to the door, hand him the initiative12 — with the pacing adjusted for a dog who is also doing crate work and stranger work in the same fortnight. Five minutes a day. It fits in the Module 14 table without displacing anything.

Teach the touch, away from the door

Living room, no door involved. Hold the target — your hand, the felt square, the muffled bell — a few inches from his nose. Dogs investigate what is offered; the instant his nose makes contact, mark it and pay.1 Five to ten reps, then stop. Two or three of these sessions a day, none longer than a couple of minutes.

Move on when: ten out of ten touches, no hesitation

Name it, then move it

Add a word as he is already committing to the touch, not before. Then start moving the target: lower, higher, in your other hand, held against a wall. You are teaching touch this thing, not touch this thing in this one spot.

Move on when: he touches it wherever you hold it

Mount it by the door, and pair it with leaving

Fix the target at nose height by the door you actually use. Now the pairing: before every single potty trip, you cue the touch, he touches, the door opens.1 Every trip, no exceptions, for several days. The association you are building is touch → door opens, and it is built entirely by your consistency, not by his cleverness.

Move on when: he touches it as you approach the door, before you ask

Pay outside, where it counts

Three pieces of food the instant he finishes, outside, while he is still standing there — the same rule Module 13 gives for house-training, for the same reason. Paying him when he comes back in pays the wrong thing.

Move on when: the outside payment is automatic for you

Go quiet and wait

Stop cueing. Keep taking him out on the Module 13 schedule, but stop asking for the touch first — just stand near the door and wait a few seconds. Sooner or later he offers it. That is the whole game: the first unprompted touch. Mark it, pay it, open the door immediately.2

Move on when: he has offered it unprompted three or four times

Honour it, every time, for a month

For the first several weeks, an unprompted signal always gets the door. Always. This is what teaches him the channel works. Ignoring it twice on a busy Tuesday teaches him it doesn't, and he will go back to circling — or to the rug.

You are done when: he asks, you open, and neither of you thinks about it
The one rule that does most of the work

A signalled trip is a working trip. Leash on, straight to the spot, a few minutes, back in. No play, no sniffing tour, no adventure.3 Adventures are wonderful and he needs them — they just must never begin with him touching the target, or you have taught him that the target produces fun, and he will produce it all evening.4

When it goes wrong

He signals constantly

The commonest failure, and it is not disobedience: he has correctly learned that the target opens the door, and going outside is interesting.34 The fix is not to stop answering — that breaks the channel you just built. The fix is to make the answer boring.

  • Leash, spot, two minutes, back in. Nothing else happens.3
  • If he asks within fifteen minutes of a trip where he produced nothing, take him anyway, on leash, two minutes, no commentary.4 The boredom does the teaching.
  • If it is clearly boredom rather than bladder, the answer is a Kong, a scatter feed, or a game — the Module 12 library exists for this. A dog asking to go out forty times is often a dog whose day is underbuilt.3

He signals, goes out, and does nothing

Back to the top of this page. Either he is asking for something other than a bladder (see above), or outside doesn't feel safe enough to relax in.5 If it is the second, stop the signal work and go back to making trips boring and quiet — the quietest window you can manage, shortest route, the twenty-minute shift Module 13 recommends. You cannot train a signal for an event he is braced against.

He stops signalling

Almost always because it stopped working — a few missed asks, or the trips got scary again. Rebuild by going back to step three: cue the touch before every trip for several days, pay outside, then go quiet again. It comes back faster the second time.

Not this

No correction, ever, for any of it — not for over-asking, not for accidents. Module 13 already says it: punishment around elimination teaches dogs to go where you cannot see them, which is strictly worse than the problem you started with. With a fearful dog it also costs you the thing this whole page is trying to build, which is his willingness to tell you something.

The fortnight, concretely

Assumes the gate at the top is clear. Five minutes a day, folded into the schedule you already run.

Expectations

Most dogs learn the mechanical part in days — touching a thing for food is easy.2 The part that takes weeks is him generalising it to I have a full bladder, therefore I will go and do that thing. That is a much bigger inference than it looks, and it arrives suddenly rather than gradually. Bad days are data, not failure.
