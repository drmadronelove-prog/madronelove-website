California · Alameda County

You don’t adopt a foster child. You foster one.

And sometimes — not usually, but sometimes — that child stays.

Every honest guide to foster-to-adopt has to start there, because almost every dishonest one starts somewhere else. Here is the actual sequence, the actual clock, and the actual phone number.

9 steps ~12 min read Updated August 2026

“Foster-to-adopt” is not a program you enroll in. It’s a description of what happens when a family says yes to fostering, the court eventually rules out going home, and the person the child already lives with turns out to be you.

You can’t apply for the last part. You can only be standing there when it happens.

Before anything else

The math nobody puts on the brochure

~60,000

children in California foster care

The majority have a case plan aimed at going home to their parents. Your job, for most of any placement, is to help that succeed.

15,386

waiting to be adopted

These are the children whose parents’ rights have already ended. They skew older, they’re often in sibling groups, and they wait a long time.

Reality check

If your goal is specifically to adopt, and you’re open to a child who is nine rather than nine months, ask the county about children already legally free. That’s a shorter, more certain road. Foster-to-adopt is the longer one, and it asks you to want something for a family that isn’t yours.

Part one · Getting approved

Nine steps to a placement

California folded foster licensing, guardianship approval, and adoption home studies into one process in 2017. It’s called Resource Family Approval, and clearing it once clears you for all three.

01

Go to an orientation

Time: one eveningCost: $0

Two hours in a room, free, no commitment. Alameda County runs these regularly. Go to one before you decide anything — it will tell you more than any website, including this one.

Bring one question that would end the whole idea if answered badly. Ask it out loud.

02

Apply, and get fingerprinted

Time: 2–4 weeksCost: ~$100–300

The application is form RFA-01A plus a stack of documents: ID, proof of income, proof of residence, a DMV record, a health questionnaire, and character references from people who’ll actually pick up the phone.

Then background checks — and they are thorough:

  • Live Scan fingerprinting against state and federal criminal records
  • The Child Abuse Central Index
  • Sex offender registry and licensing databases
  • Everyone in the household over 18, not just you
Old record?

Not automatically disqualifying. There’s a formal criminal record exemption process, and it turns on what the offense was and how long ago. Disclose it yourself at the start. Being found out later is what ends applications.

03

Pass the home inspection

Time: one visitCost: whatever you have to fix

This is where more applications stall than anywhere else, almost always over bedrooms. The rules that actually catch people:

  • A room used for anything else isn’t a bedroom. Not the dining room, not the hallway, not the garage, and not a living room with a partition across it. This applies to your room too, so you can’t sleep behind a curtain and give the child the bedroom.
  • Every bedroom needs a direct emergency exit to the outside — a window or door that opens.
  • Children over two don’t share a bedroom with an adult. Infants can: up to two of them may sleep in your room.
  • Children can share with each other — up to four of the same gender identity per room. A private bedroom is not required.
  • Bunk beds max two tiers, rails on top, nobody under six up there.
  • Firearms locked and stored separately from ammunition. Pools fenced. Medications and chemicals locked.
The one-bedroom problem

If you live in a one-bedroom, you are not out — you’re limited to an infant. Everything else needs a real second bedroom with walls, a door, and a window. A permitted conversion counts. A bookshelf does not.

04

Do the training

Time: ~12 hours + a CPR classCost: usually free

Twelve hours of pre-approval training is the state floor; Alameda runs about twelve and a half. Plus pediatric CPR and first aid, which you’ll do at a Red Cross or community college session.

The training is mostly about trauma — what it does to a developing brain, why a child might rage over a closed door or hoard food in a house that has plenty. It is the most useful part of the entire process and the part people most want to skip.

05

Sit through the family evaluation

Time: 2+ interviews, one at home

A social worker interviews you at least twice, once in your house, watching how your household actually runs. They ask about your childhood, your discipline philosophy, your relationships, your money, your mental health, your substance use, your losses.

They’re assessing one thing above all: can you work with a birth family? Not tolerate — work with. Drive a child to a visit with a parent you have feelings about, and hand them over at the door without making it heavy.

How to do this well

Don’t perform. Evaluators have read a thousand of these and can smell a curated answer. Say the true thing, including the parts where you’re not sure. "I don’t know how I’d handle that" is a better answer than a polished one.

06

Get approved

Statutory clock: 90 calendar daysRealistic: 3–6 months total

By law the county has 90 days from your application to finish the written assessment, absent good cause. In practice the whole thing — orientation to approval — usually runs three to six months, and the delays are almost always paperwork you owe them, not the other way around.

Approval doesn’t guarantee a placement. It makes you eligible for one.

07

Take the call

Notice: sometimes hours

A placement call gives you a first name, an age, a few sentences about why the child was removed, and a decision window that can be very short. You will not get a complete history, because nobody has one yet.

You’re allowed to say no. Say it when the answer is no — a placement that breaks down is worse for the child than one that never started. But notice whether you’re saying no to this child or to the fact that it’s suddenly real.

Part two · The clock you don’t control

What happens in dependency court

From the day a child is removed, a statutory clock runs. Everything about whether you eventually adopt is decided on this timeline, by a judge, mostly without you in the room.

California dependency timeline

Measured from the child’s removal. Reunification is the goal at every mark until it isn’t. The green span is where most cases land.

Scroll the timeline sideways →
Days 1–60 Petition, detention hearing, jurisdiction, disposition. The court decides the child was unsafe at home and orders reunification services.
Month 6 First review. For children under three, services can end here — the shortest clock in the system.
Month 12 Permanency hearing. The court must return the child unless returning is shown to be detrimental.
Month 18 Permanency review. Extending past here takes strong findings; 24 months is the outer limit.
+120 days The .26 hearing. Once services are terminated, this is set within 120 days — and adoption is the first option the court must consider.
Median time in care in California: 12–20 months. Most of that, you are a foster parent, not a prospective adoptive one.
The part that hurts

Reunification succeeding is the system working. You will be asked to help it succeed — to show up at visits, speak well of a parent in front of their kid, keep a bedroom that a child might leave. Some people can hold that and grieve it at the same time. Some can’t. Both are honest; only one should foster.

Part three · If the answer becomes adoption

The last two steps

08

The .26 hearing

Named for Welfare & Institutions Code §366.26. The judge picks a permanent plan from a ranked list, and adoption sits at the top — guardianship and long-term placement are what the court chooses when adoption isn’t possible.

If adoption is selected, parental rights are terminated at this hearing. California law gives strong preference to the family the child is already living with. That’s the whole quiet logic of foster-to-adopt: you don’t get chosen at the end, you get chosen because you were already there.

09

Finalize

Time: a few months after TPRCost: $0–500

Because your RFA approval already covers adoption, there’s no second home study. An adoption assessment is completed, a petition is filed, and you go to court one last time — a hearing that takes about fifteen minutes and is, by a wide margin, the happiest thing that happens in a dependency courtroom.

People bring their whole family. The judge lets the kid bang the gavel. Take the day off.

Part five

Six things people wish they’d known

01 · The honeymoon ends

The first few weeks are often eerily good. Children who’ve learned that adults are unpredictable are careful before they’re comfortable. The behavior that shows up in week six isn’t regression — it’s trust.

02 · You’ll be managed, not consulted

Social workers change. Court dates move. You’ll learn things about your own household from a hearing report. Keep your own written log of dates, appointments, and incidents from day one; you will need it and nobody will ask you for it until you do.

03 · Discipline rules are different

No physical discipline of any kind, ever. There are rules about where the child can sleep, who can babysit, and whether they can leave the county. It’s a real loss of autonomy and it surprises people.

04 · Siblings are the open door

The children waiting longest are older kids and sibling groups. If you can take two, you will be placed faster and you will be doing the harder, more needed thing.

05 · Find your people before you need them

Foster parent support groups exist in every county and are full of people who’ve been through exactly the week you’re having. Join one during approval, not during a crisis.

06 · Say yes to the first placement anyway

Most families’ first placement is not the child they adopt. It’s the one that teaches them how to do this. That’s not a wasted year — it’s a child who got a safe place while their family got itself back.

Start here

What to actually do Monday

  1. Call the county. Ask for the next orientation date and say plainly that you’re interested in foster-to-adopt. They’ll tell you the truth about wait times and current need.
  2. Measure your bedrooms. Confirm each one has walls, a door, and a window that opens. This determines what ages you can be approved for, and everything else follows from it.
  3. Write down your no. Ages, behaviors, medical needs, sibling groups. Being specific now protects a child from a placement that breaks down later.
